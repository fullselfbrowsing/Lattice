## Reference narrative

Organizational accountability depends on more than retaining an AI answer. A reviewer needs to know which information was available, which execution constraints applied, what the system returned, and what the record leaves unestablished. The public refund decisions illustrate these distinctions. A payment order is not proof of settlement; a stated amount can conflict with other figures in the same source; and an apparently correct answer can point to a page that does not support it. The design problem is therefore to make evidence inspectable while preserving uncertainty about its truth, completeness and organizational meaning.

The five requirements organize that problem using governance sources and the artifact's existing evidence. This mapping was refined retrospectively. It does not imply that the requirements were elicited from practitioners or registered before the experiments. The EU AI Act supplies conditional motivation through its provisions on logging, transparency, human oversight and retained logs for high-risk systems. Applicability depends on the system's intended use and other legal conditions; an ordinary refund-review task is not automatically high-risk. These provisions also do not prescribe signed receipts. Cryptographic commitments are a design choice for addressing some evidence needs, not proof that an organization satisfies the Act.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 provides complementary, voluntary guidance. Its selected outcomes concern documented responsibilities, differentiated human and AI roles, knowledge limits, evaluation documentation, accountability and privacy risks, costs, and operational responses. They support a broader organizational interpretation of the requirements while revealing what the implementation does not evaluate. A technical issuer and an accountable manager are different actors. Recording a failed format check is useful evidence, but it does not demonstrate an effective appeal process. An organization still needs people with appropriate authority and procedures for acting on the information.

DR1 concerns integrity and attribution to an issuer key under stated trust assumptions. Its evidence comes from defined mutation and verification checks, rather than a claim that every possible alteration or missing event is detectable. DR2 concerns verifying retained evidence. Recomputing a hash can establish consistency with a recorded commitment when the relevant bytes and trusted expectations are available. It cannot regenerate a model's reasoning or recover an absent source. A failed response can have a valid terminal signature while lacking a successful-output commitment, so signature validity and evidence completeness must be reported separately.

DR3 concerns inspectable execution and explicit control outcomes. Deterministic provider selection can be checked when the relevant request, policy and catalog are retained. That is different from explaining model reasoning or authorizing a refund. Likewise, response format, factual accuracy and supporting-page coverage require different checks. The public-record review shows why those distinctions matter: integrity verification can succeed while a response fails its format contract or a source-reference judgment needs correction. The two-way audit remains separate from the frozen score and does not alter the original model outputs.

DR4 and DR5 address the costs of making evidence available. Minimizing a receipt's contents can reduce direct disclosure, but retained source stores need their own controls, and unsalted hashes can reveal guessable values. A planted-marker scan therefore supports only a limited privacy claim. Technical cost measurements also have a defined scope: historical signing, verification and receipt-size measurements do not establish integration effort, reviewer effort or an acceptable organizational budget. No threshold is introduced after observing those results.

Together, the principles propose configured automatic signing with visible failures, retained content and required commitments, deterministic routing with recorded control outcomes, and minimized disclosure with verification before artifact loading. The traceability table connects these proposals to specific evidence and remaining gaps. The completed work provides technical and retrospective document-review evidence. Demonstrating that the design helps managers investigate disputes still requires evaluation with practitioners in an organizational setting.
