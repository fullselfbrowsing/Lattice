## Reference narrative

### Business setting and responsibilities

A contested refund makes organizational responsibility concrete. A customer asks why a request was rejected; the reviewing employee must explain the decision, and a manager must determine whether the applicable terms and the company's own communications support it. The motivating real-world example is *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, where the tribunal considered conflicting chatbot and webpage advice and held the company responsible for information on its website. The decision also identified missing contractual evidence. It does not establish a tampering incident, identify the chatbot as a large language model, or show that Lattice would have prevented the dispute.[1]

The demonstration therefore uses a fictional travel provider with invented bookings, policies and correspondence. It is neither a replication of that judgment nor a deployment at Air Canada or another named company. The customer submits a request or challenge. An employee reviews routine cases within delegated authority; a manager handles policy conflicts and higher-value exceptions. The application operator retains the evidence and configures Lattice. A separate payment service is represented by a simulator. Only the model recommendations and technical verification are live computations; employee and manager actions are fixtures, and no funds move.[2]

### From request to recommendation and review

The application supplies the booking, customer request and versioned refund terms as structured artifacts. A disputed case also includes a company refund promise and a record of the earlier rejection. Lattice runs the task through a pinned free model, checks the recommendation's format and produces a signed receipt. The application retains the exact artifacts, output and serialized provider request. Verification compares these records with the receipt's commitments and checks the expected task and execution policy. The business refund terms are an input artifact: they are distinct from Lattice's execution policy governing provider and privacy constraints.[2]

In the ordinary case, an unused flexible booking costing USD 180 was cancelled before departure. The invented policy permits employee approval up to USD 200. Both models recommended approval in both repetitions. The application independently checked the recommendation against the booking and business rules, then consumed a simulated employee approval for USD 180. The payment fixture recorded one payment and blocked a second attempt. This demonstrates the separation between a recommendation, an approval record and a payment record; it does not demonstrate authenticated human authorization or durable payment processing.[2,3]

The exception concerns a USD 240 basic fare. Its terms exclude a routine refund, but a company-support message promises one. The earlier denial and customer challenge are explicitly scripted backstory, not observations from an earlier model run. In all four live reviews, the models identified the conflict and recommended manager escalation with USD 0 immediately authorized. A separate manager-approval fixture then authorized USD 240 under the invented service-recovery limit of USD 300. The application checked the amount and role before simulating payment. Missing evidence and mismatched booking identifiers instead produced requests for evidence, without payment.[2,3]

### What the recorded evidence establishes

A reviewer with the saved public key can verify the issuer's signature and compare retained content with signed commitments. In this demonstration, a fresh process also checked task, execution-policy and serialized-request bindings. These checks help distinguish altered output, unexpected input and incomplete evidence. They do not establish that the issuer was truthful, the provider consumed the committed request, the decision was fair, or the refund terms were legally applicable. Approval and payment records require separate application checks. Their linkage in this simulator is not proof of a real employee's identity or bank settlement. Checking stored output is not re-executing the model.[2,3]

### Observed outcomes and remaining questions

The frozen cohort comprised eight scenarios, two free models and two repetitions: 32 evaluation runs, with two development calls excluded. Nemotron 3.5 Super VL and Dots 3 Note Preview each matched the expected action, immediate amount, authority, conflict flag and decisive-rule inclusion on 16/16 runs. Each passed the full rubric on 14/16 runs, for 28/32 overall. The four failures omitted the required customer-request reference: two disputed-promise runs for Dots, and one already-refunded and one customer-instruction run for Nemotron. The format contract accepted these outputs because it checked valid source identifiers, not complete required-source coverage. Explanation entailment was not systematically scored.[2,3]

All 32 retained run-evidence packages verified despite those source-list omissions. Eighteen scoped integrity and application checks passed, including intact controls; these were not 18 distinct cryptographic attacks. A separate offline control produced a deliberately wrong but validly signed recommendation. Its evidence verified, its decision score failed, and the application withheld payment. All 34 inference requests reported zero cost, with USD 0 added account usage. These observations support technical feasibility in a small, structured demonstration. They do not establish model rankings, improved managerial judgment, reduced dispute costs, regulatory compliance or superiority over secured logging. Such benefits require a separate practitioner evaluation.[2,3]
