## Reference narrative

An organization reviewing an AI-assisted decision faces a problem that spans technical records and institutional responsibility. The reviewer may need to establish which information was available, how a recommendation arose, which policy governed the action, and who authorized the outcome. These questions require different kinds of evidence. A record can be authentic yet incomplete, and a correctly retained recommendation can still be wrong. The literature review should therefore explain the relationship between organizational accountability and the narrower properties that an evidence-producing information system can supply. This distinction gives the paper a contribution that readers can assess without accepting claims that cryptography makes an organization accountable.

Organizational AI governance provides the first foundation. Berente and colleagues identify autonomy, learning and inscrutability as management concerns. Mäntymäki and colleagues connect technological tools with organizational rules, processes and values, while Schneider and colleagues distinguish the governance of data, models and systems. Papagiannidis and colleagues synthesize responsible governance through structural, procedural and relational practices. These accounts suggest a placement for Lattice: a technical component within a broader arrangement of responsibilities and practices. They do not establish that retaining a receipt fulfills those responsibilities, or that one implementation improves the organization's governance. The paper can use them to motivate its design question while keeping their conceptual claims separate from its own experimental findings.

The second foundation concerns delegation and oversight. Baird and Maruping's framework directs attention to relations between people and agentic information systems. Its relevance is that assigning a task and accepting its result are organizational choices, not merely successive software calls. Asatiani and colleagues examine how organizational and technical arrangements bound the use of inscrutable AI in a government agency. Their sociotechnical perspective helps explain why a verification mechanism needs a surrounding process for exceptions and intervention. Grisold and colleagues offer future-oriented design principles for human–AI coordination, including monitoring and intervention. These perspectives motivate proposed responsibilities; they do not turn the refund harness into an observed human oversight process.

Rinta-Kahila and colleagues provide a complementary empirical warning through their study of Australia's Robodebt programme. Their retrospective analysis concerns a destructive public-sector decision system and the organizational conditions around it. It should not be presented as evidence about generative models or as a situation that Lattice would have prevented. Its relevance is the importance of oversight and investigation arrangements when decisions are challenged. A preserved technical record may support an investigation, but the willingness and authority to question a decision, interpret evidence and provide redress remain separate. This connection lets the review speak to the refund problem without implying that all contested decisions share the same causes.

Auditing and documentation research supplies the third foundation. Raji and colleagues describe an internal auditing process with documentation across development. Mökander and colleagues distinguish governance, model and application auditing. Model cards and dataset documentation supply information at other useful levels, while provenance research describes derivation histories. A run receipt complements these materials by recording selected execution commitments. It does not replace a model's documented limitations, a dataset's provenance, or an auditor's assessment of the organization's practices. The relevant question is which records are necessary for the claim being investigated and whether the reviewer can obtain and check them. No universal ranking of evidence gaps across audit layers is needed.

Secure logging research establishes that integrity protection is an existing capability. Signed records, hash structures and transparency mechanisms differ in what they commit to, who is trusted and what omissions they can expose. Recent agent proposals add further distinctions, including receiver-side attestation and evidence recorded before effects are released. Lattice should be compared on these dimensions rather than introduced as the first way to make records verifiable. Its signed-log comparator's parity is useful evidence for this restraint. The design question concerns which run information is committed, what separately retained content is required, and which expectations the verifier must enforce. A dishonest issuer's signed assertion remains different from independent evidence that an event occurred.

The refund review makes these distinctions concrete. Signature validity, completeness of successful-output evidence, and agreement with the source-based rubric are separate outcomes. A failed run can have a valid signature without supplying the same output commitment as a successful run. A complete evidence package can preserve an incorrect answer faithfully. Supporting source references can also be inadequate even when extracted decision fields are correct. The review should connect these outcomes to the requirements without treating them as observations of employee authorization, payment execution, or successful customer redress. They demonstrate why evidence properties must be interpreted together rather than collapsed into one pass rate.

Design science provides the final connection between the literature and the artifact. Requirements and principles make the intended design knowledge explicit; an implementation and its evaluation expose where that knowledge is supported and where it remains tentative. Lattice contributes a concrete combination of mechanisms and lessons about evidence boundaries. The completed technical experiments assess defined integrity checks, retained-output verification, execution controls and measured cost components. They do not establish acceptable total organizational cost or improved investigation performance. A later practitioner study would need to examine whether people can identify missing evidence, reach sound conclusions and conduct reviews efficiently. Until then, the organizational value is a design objective supported by a reasoned literature connection, not a demonstrated outcome.
