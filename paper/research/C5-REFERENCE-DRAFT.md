## Reference narrative

For a manager, the value of a recorded AI recommendation depends on the questions it can answer during a dispute. What information was available? Which policy applied? What did the model recommend? Who authorized the action, and what actually happened? A run receipt can support some of these questions, provided the organization retains the underlying material. It does not combine them into a single guarantee. The public refund decisions illustrate why approval, temporary credit, a direction to pay and completed settlement need distinct records.

The first adoption decision is responsibility. A business-process owner should define the approval policy, authority limits, escalation route and process for customer contestation. A reviewing employee should inspect the recommendation and its source support, while an authorized manager records any approval or exception. The application operator and security custodian should manage signing configuration, trusted keys and responses to missing receipts. A data owner should set retention, access and retrieval arrangements. Independent review should examine both the technical evidence and separate business records. These are proposed assignments, not roles tested in the study.

The refund review shows why these responsibilities cannot be delegated to signature verification. Every terminal signature verified, including the signature associated with an unusable response. Complete successful-output evidence was available for fewer runs, and the source-based rubric had a separate outcome. A manager should therefore see verification status, evidence completeness and substantive review findings separately. An incomplete packet should remain visibly incomplete. A signing failure needs an explicit organizational response; it should not disappear merely because an application produced an answer.

Retention is a prerequisite for meaningful inspection. The organization needs source material, applicable business-policy versions, the execution task and policy, serialized requests, model outputs and receipts. Approvals and payment confirmations require their own records. Where routing decisions matter, the relevant configuration and catalog evidence must also remain available. A digest permits comparison with retained content; it cannot recover content that has been deleted. The retention design should identify who may retrieve each record, how access is controlled and what remains verifiable after a deletion. Hashes alone do not settle those choices or ensure privacy.

Adoption should also begin with an assessment of existing controls. The matched signed-log comparator achieved the same recorded integrity outcomes as Lattice. An organization already retaining suitable signed records may not need another record format. The design opportunity is to produce consistent commitments during execution and carry their verification into later review. That opportunity should be assessed against the work required to integrate the mechanism, administer keys, retain content and investigate exceptions. The measured processing costs do not estimate those organizational costs or establish an acceptable budget.

A staged pilot could test that opportunity without treating technical verification as permission to automate refunds. First, reviewers could inspect retained historical records, checking whether the packet answers predefined dispute questions. This stage would verify stored evidence rather than attempt to reproduce model behavior. Second, shadow operation could produce packets alongside an existing workflow while leaving established approval controls in place. Third, a limited operational pilot could proceed only after organizational approval of responsibilities, evidence requirements and exception handling. Each stage is a proposal; none has been conducted as a practitioner evaluation.

Prospective measures should distinguish the stages and their denominators. Evidence completeness could be the proportion of eligible cases containing every required record. Verification outcomes should separate failed signatures, missing content, mismatched expectations and signing failures. Escalation measures could record whether predefined exceptions reached an authorized reviewer and how they were resolved. Investigation accuracy would require an independently reviewed reference, including explicit unknowns. Review time and integration effort should be measured separately, with comparable tasks and the existing logging workflow as a baseline. The organization should set acceptance criteria before collecting pilot outcomes rather than infer thresholds from the present experiments.

Any participant evaluation would need the applicable institutional determination, consent arrangements and author approval before recruitment. Until such evidence exists, faster investigation, better decisions and improved customer outcomes remain proposed benefits. Lattice supplies a technical means of retaining and checking specified commitments; the organization remains responsible for the records around them, the authority to act and the judgment exercised when evidence conflicts.
