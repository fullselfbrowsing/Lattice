\begin{abstract}
\noindent Organizations remain responsible for reviewing and justifying AI-assisted business decisions, yet a retained answer alone may not establish the integrity of the evidence behind it. Motivated by accountability in refund disputes, this design-science study develops five requirements and four principles for verifiable run-level evidence and instantiates them in Lattice, an open-source runtime. Signed results can carry receipts that bind outputs and selected execution information; optional commitments cover tasks, policies, and serialized provider requests. Historical controlled evaluation tested tamper detection, retained-output verification, policy checks, and signing overhead; separate hosted cohorts examine execution and answer quality, including retrospective review of four actual public refund-dispute records. These evaluations do not establish improved managerial decisions or reduced dispute-resolution costs. Verification checks recorded commitments, not provider honesty, decision correctness, fairness, or human authorization. Stored-output verification is not model re-execution, and request commitments do not prove remote consumption. The contribution is a design for producing evidence during execution, with explicit limits on what that evidence supports. Its usefulness to managers and auditors remains to be evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Consider an illustrative refund dispute. A customer challenges a rejection recommended by an artificial intelligence (AI) system and authorized by an employee. The customer-service manager responsible for resolving the complaint must establish which policy applied, what information the system received, what it recommended, and why the employee accepted that recommendation. If the retained record contains only the final answer, the manager cannot reconstruct those steps from that record alone. The organizational problem is how to preserve evidence that lets responsible people investigate and justify a decision. This example motivates the study; it is not a report of a deployed refund process.

In such a workflow, one model could interpret a document, another could recommend an outcome, and a tool could retrieve policy information before an employee approves or escalates the case. These contributions distribute the work, while the organization still needs an accountable decision owner and a review process. Organizational AI governance connects technical processes with organizational objectives and responsibilities \citep{mantymaki2022}. For the post-AI enterprise, the resulting challenge concerns human oversight and contestability: a manager must be able to question an AI-assisted outcome against the organization's rules, rather than treat the availability of an answer as sufficient justification.

Meeting that need requires more than collecting outputs. A debugging trace may record a request and response without binding them to retained inputs, the selected provider, or the policy configuration. If its integrity is not protected, a reviewer also lacks evidence that the record remained unchanged. Secure and tamper-evident logging already address important parts of this problem \citep{crosby2009}. The design gap examined here is how to apply such protections to the evidence associated with an individual model run, while making the scope of each commitment clear. Auditing research likewise treats documentation as part of an organizational process, not a substitute for one \citep{raji2020}.

Governance frameworks reinforce the need for evidence. Article~12 of the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act addresses automatic event recording for high-risk AI systems \citep{euaiact2024}. The voluntary NIST AI Risk Management Framework connects documentation and monitoring with organizational risk management \citep{nist2023}. These frameworks motivate the requirements examined below; they do not establish that the illustrative refund workflow is legally high-risk or that a signed record demonstrates compliance. Organizations must still determine which obligations apply and how records support their review procedures.

This study addresses the evidence problem through design science research (DSR) \citep{hevner2004}. The research question is: \emph{How can AI-enabled business processes produce verifiable run-level evidence that supports organizational review and accountability at acceptable operational cost?} We derive requirements from governance and auditing literature, formulate design principles, and instantiate them in Lattice, an open-source runtime \citep{lattice}. Its central construct is the \emph{capability receipt}: a signed record of a run's selected execution information and content commitments. The design allows reviewers to check the integrity of retained evidence offline. Producing evidence useful to managers is a design objective whose achievement requires evaluation with those users.

The study makes three contributions: requirements and principles for run-level evidence, an implementation evaluated for tamper detection, recorded-output binding, policy checks and overhead, and lessons about the limits of that evidence. Following the distinction between an artifact and its knowledge contribution \citep{gregor2013}, the transferable contribution concerns which claims a record can support and which require additional controls. Controlled and hosted evaluations supply technical evidence; they do not demonstrate improved managerial decisions or faster resolution of customer disputes.

A valid receipt establishes integrity of the signed commitments. It does not establish that an issuing runtime or provider acted honestly, that a decision was correct or fair, or that an employee authorized it. Those questions require additional evidence and organizational judgment. Verification of retained outputs is also distinct from re-executing a model. These boundaries matter when deciding how much reliance to place on a record during review.

Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews related work; Section~\ref{sec:method} explains the research approach. Sections~\ref{sec:requirements}, \ref{sec:refund-case} and~\ref{sec:artifact} present requirements, the public-record refund review and the artifact. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates it, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AI accountability, governance, and auditing}

Accountability in AI denotes the obligation of an actor to explain and justify the conduct of a system to a forum that can pass judgment and impose consequences \citep{wieringa2020,novelli2023}. IS research frames AI management as a matter of autonomy, learning, and inscrutability \citep{berente2021}, and it describes how organizations envelop AI with sociotechnical controls to contain its behavior \citep{asatiani2021}. Organizational AI governance comprises the structures, processes, and procedures that align AI use with organizational and regulatory goals \citep{mantymaki2022,schneider2022,papagiannidis2025}. Across these accounts, governance depends on evidence. Policies and controls mean little unless an organization can show, for specific decisions, that the controls operated.

The AI auditing literature makes this dependence concrete. \citet{raji2020} propose an end-to-end internal audit framework and stress documentation artifacts throughout the development life cycle. \citet{mokander2021} argue for ethics-based auditing as a governance mechanism, and \citet{mokander2023} propose a three-layered approach to auditing large language models at the governance, model, and application layers. \citet{brundage2020} call for mechanisms, including audit trails, that make claims about AI development verifiable by outsiders. Transparency research cautions that transparency must be designed for specific audiences and purposes \citep{felzmann2019,rai2020}, and human-centered AI calls for reliable, safe, and trustworthy systems backed by audit trails \citep{shneiderman2020}. Documentation instruments such as model cards \citep{mitchell2019} and datasheets for datasets \citep{gebru2021} standardize disclosure about models and data. However, they describe artifacts at release time, not individual production runs. The application layer of \citet{mokander2023}, where a model is used in a specific business process, is where the evidence gap is widest, and it is the layer this study targets.

\subsection{Tamper-evident logging, provenance, and transparency}

Computer security offers a long tradition of tamper-evident records. \citet{haber1991} showed how to time-stamp digital documents, and \citet{merkle1988} introduced hash-tree commitments. \citet{schneier1999} designed secure audit logs whose entries cannot be undetectably altered after compromise, and \citet{crosby2009} gave efficient data structures for tamper-evident logs with logarithmic proofs. Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013} publishes certificates to append-only logs so that misissuance becomes publicly detectable. Software supply chain research applies these ideas to builds. in-toto \citep{intoto} signs each step of a software supply chain, The Update Framework addresses survivable key compromise \citep{samuel2010}, Sigstore makes signing and transparency logging accessible to developers \citep{newman2022}, and SLSA defines levels of supply chain integrity \citep{slsa}. The Dead Simple Signing Envelope (DSSE) specifies a minimal signed envelope with a pre-authentication encoding \citep{dsse}. Data provenance research models the derivation history of data products \citep{moreau2011,herschel2017}.

These lines of work supply mechanisms (canonical encodings, signatures, hash chains, transparency logs) but were not designed for the semantics of a model run. A model run has a declared intent, a routing decision among providers, policy constraints, output invariants, and multimodal inputs that may contain personal data. Model gateways and observability libraries such as LiteLLM \citep{litellm}, LangChain callbacks \citep{langchain}, OpenLLMetry \citep{openllmetry}, and the Portkey gateway \citep{portkey} sit on that path and log the calls, but none of the four signs its log, and the logs that could be read stored the prompt text. Section~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} reports these logs and the wrappers' latency on a CPU-only host, where generation noise dominates.

\subsection{Verifiable inference and model routing}

A complementary stream seeks cryptographic guarantees that a specific model computed a specific output, using zero-knowledge proofs \citep{kang2022zk,sun2024}. Such proofs give strong guarantees but currently require access to model weights and impose high costs, so they do not apply to proprietary hosted models consumed through application programming interfaces (APIs). Work on model routing optimizes cost and quality by selecting among models \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm}, often with learned routers whose decisions are hard to audit. Security research on LLM-integrated applications shows that model outputs can be manipulated through indirect prompt injection \citep{greshake2023}, which raises the stakes for run-level evidence.

\subsection{Agent receipts and verifiable agent provenance}

Three recent proposals are closest to this study. \emph{TrustTrack} is a vision paper that argues for verifiable identity, policy commitments, and tamper-resistant behavioral logs in multi-agent systems \citep{li2025trusttrack}. \emph{Sello} (``Notarized Agents'') inverts the trust boundary: the service that receives an agent's call signs a receipt, encrypts it to the agent's owner, and publishes it to a witness-cosigned Merkle log, so that the owner need not trust the agent or its operator \citep{figuera2026sello}. \emph{Janus} places a signed, hash-chained log on the effect path of agentic LLMs, so that a step's proposal and verdict are durable before its effect is released. Its gates are pure functions of the log and can be re-derived offline, and it is evaluated under crash injection and with a lending workflow \citep{arslan2026janus}. Two further systems are run in the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:realworld} but are not tabulated here: Agent Flight Recorder, which keeps tamper-evident audit trails of tool-using agents with on-chain anchoring \citep{bindschaedler2026afr}, and NovaFabric, which seals tamper-evident, replayable evidence for autonomous agent runs \citep{ardebili2026novafabric}.

Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares these systems with the artifact presented here. The contribution is a combination of features aimed at \emph{organizational} model use: issuer-side receipts for signed calls, terminal single-agent results, and crews; deterministic routing with a signed selected route; redaction before signing; and content commitments. Lattice still lacks Sello's receiver-side attestation and transparency log and Janus's effect gating and crash-consistency evaluation. Its current optional task, policy, and request bindings remain issuer claims (Section~\ref{sec:artifact}). Lattice was designed independently. Receipt code first appears in the project repository \citep{lattice} in commit \texttt{fa6d5a1} of 11 May 2026, before the Sello (June 2026) and Janus (September 2026) preprints appeared. Audience, nonce, and expiry binding, chain verification, chain closure, and the later execution-binding extensions were added in October 2026, after both.

\section{Research Approach}\label{sec:method}

This study follows the design science research methodology (DSRM) of \citet{peffers2007}, read through the guidelines of \citet{hevner2004} and the account of design contributions in \citet{gregor2013}. Design science builds artifacts and prescriptive knowledge for practical problems \citep{walls1992,vombrocke2020}. The problem here is that organizations remain answerable for AI-assisted decisions without dependable evidence of what each model run did. Following \citet{gregor2007}, we separate three levels: requirements for the class of systems that should produce such evidence, principles of form and function for meeting those requirements, and Lattice as one instantiation.

Table~\ref{tab:dsrm} maps the six DSRM activities to the work reported here. DSRM allows research to enter the process at different points \citep{peffers2007}. This project entered at design and development: the runtime was built before the requirements took their present form. The requirements in Section~\ref{sec:requirements} are therefore a retrospective refinement against governance, auditing and secure-logging sources. They are not the product of stakeholder elicitation, and no acceptance threshold was fixed before the measurements were taken. This limits what the evaluation can confirm. It tests whether the design meets requirements articulated after the fact, not whether practitioners would have chosen those requirements.

Evaluation follows the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS), which classifies evaluation episodes by purpose (formative or summative) and setting (artificial or naturalistic) \citep{venable2016}. The principal design risks are technical: whether records resist tampering, bind the intended content, and cost little to produce. We therefore follow the framework's Technical Risk and Efficacy strategy, which establishes efficacy in artificial settings before moving to naturalistic ones. The study reports three kinds of episode:
\begin{itemize}
\item Controlled tests with fixtures and a simulated provider are artificial and summative for the revisions they measured; later code does not inherit their results.
\item Live-provider experiments use real hosted models but remain artificial, because no organization acted on the outputs.
\item The review of four actual dispute decisions (Section~\ref{sec:refund-case}) uses real records but ran in a research harness without a reviewer. It is a demonstration with technical evaluation, not a naturalistic episode.
\end{itemize}
The naturalistic episode, in which developers and auditors use the evidence, is planned (Section~\ref{sec:eval-study}). Evaluation criteria follow \citet{prat2015}: goal attainment, efficiency, robustness and consistency, applied within the build--evaluate cycle described by \citet{sonnenberg2012}.

\section{Design Requirements and Principles}\label{sec:requirements}

\subsection{Design requirements}

The requirements draw on three kinds of source. Governance instruments indicate what evidence organizations may need; auditing and accountability research explains why; and secure-logging research shows how the integrity of records can be protected. Two of these sources need care.

The first is the EU AI Act, whose record-keeping (Article~12), transparency to deployers (Article~13), human oversight (Article~14) and log-retention (Articles~19 and~26) duties apply only to high-risk systems \citep{euai2026text}. A system is high-risk because of its intended use under Article~6 and Annex~III, not because it handles refunds. Refund handling is not itself a listed use. A system that an ombudsman or tribunal used to interpret facts and apply rules could be high-risk, however, because Annex~III point~8(a) extends to alternative dispute resolution. Timing also matters. As amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, these obligations apply to Annex~III systems only from 2~December 2027 \citep[Art.~113]{euai2026text}. The obligation that already applies to a customer-facing refund chatbot is narrower: under Article~50(1), people must be told that they are interacting with an AI system.

The second is the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which is voluntary \citep[p.~2]{nist2023}. We use both instruments to identify evidence needs, not as the source of our mechanisms. Neither prescribes signatures, hashes or deterministic routing, and nothing in this study establishes high-risk classification or compliance.

The public records in Section~\ref{sec:refund-case} show what the requirements must preserve. A reviewer needs to know whether a record has changed since it was made, whether it can be checked against the material it describes, which controls ran and what they found, how much the record discloses, and what it costs to produce. Each question corresponds to one requirement below. The same records show what no requirement can settle. A tribunal's order to pay is not evidence of payment, and an extracted figure can be faithful to its page while the source contradicts itself. The requirements therefore ask for evidence that can be checked and leave its truth, completeness and organizational meaning to review.

\textbf{DR1 (Record integrity and issuer attribution).} A reviewer can detect any change to the content that a record commits to and can attribute the record to an issuer's trusted key. Article~12's call for automatic logs that support traceability \citep[Art.~12(1--2)]{euai2026text} and the framework's outcomes on documented responsibilities and accountability risk \citep[GOVERN~2.1, MEASURE~2.8]{nist2023} motivate the need. Secure-logging and verifiable-claims research supplies the means \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009,brundage2020}. \emph{Assessment:} intact records verify under a trusted key, and each defined mutation of content, signature or key is rejected. A key identifies an issuer, not an accountable person. Integrity establishes neither that the issuer was honest nor that the record is fresh or the history complete.

\textbf{DR2 (Verifiable retained evidence).} A reviewer can check retained inputs, outputs and required execution context against the record's commitments without calling the original provider. The Act's provisions on interpreting and retaining logs \citep[Arts.~13(3)(f), 19(1), 26(6)]{euai2026text} and the framework's call to document test sets, metrics and tools \citep[MEASURE~2.1]{nist2023} motivate the need. \emph{Assessment:} recomputed hashes match the commitments when the retained bytes are present. A missing commitment or missing content is reported as incomplete, even when the signature verifies. This is verification of retained evidence, not re-execution of the model; the reproducibility of model behavior is a separate concern \citep{raji2020,kapoor2023}.

\textbf{DR3 (Inspectable execution and explicit control outcomes).} A reviewer can see which provider and model ran, under which execution policy and routing context, and what each configured check concluded. Recomputing the selection requires the request, configuration and capability catalog. The Act's transparency and oversight provisions \citep[Arts.~13--14, 26(2)]{euai2026text} and the framework's outcomes on differentiated human and AI roles, knowledge limits, and appeal and override \citep[GOVERN~3.2, MAP~2.2, MANAGE~4.1]{nist2023} motivate the need. So do the inscrutability of AI in organizations \citep{berente2021} and the audience-specific nature of explanation \citep{felzmann2019}. \emph{Assessment:} recomputed routes match an oracle, configured invariants produce their recorded outcomes, and format, factual and source checks are reported separately. A passed execution check is not business authorization, factual correctness or human oversight.

\textbf{DR4 (Minimized disclosure).} Records shared for review disclose only what the review requires, and privacy risks are assessed for both the receipt and the retained content \citep{felzmann2019}. The framework likewise calls for privacy risk to be examined and documented \citep[MEASURE~2.10]{nist2023}. \emph{Assessment:} decoded signed payloads contain none of a set of planted sensitive markers. That test covers one narrow property. It does not establish anonymization, access control, lawful retention or the absence of unrecognized personal data, and unsalted commitments can reveal values that are easy to guess.

\textbf{DR5 (Assessable operational and adoption costs).} An organization can measure what it costs to produce, retain and check the evidence, and so judge whether that cost is acceptable for its process. The framework asks organizations to examine monetary and non-monetary costs \citep[MAP~3.2]{nist2023}, and engineering research shows how integration and maintenance burdens accumulate \citep{sculley2015,amershi2019}. \emph{Assessment:} signing and verification latency and receipt size, reported with environment and revision. We set no threshold after seeing the results. Integration effort, review effort and organizational acceptability remain untested.

\subsection{Design principles}

Each principle names an action, a mechanism and a rationale \citep{gregor2013}. Whether a principle is realized depends on configuration, the evidence available and the execution path tested. Table~\ref{tab:dp-mechanism} lists the mechanisms.

\textbf{DP1 (Configured automatic signing with explicit failures).} When a signer is configured, attempt a receipt for every supported terminal outcome, and surface signing failures through the chosen error policy. Missing receipts then become visible rather than silent, and developers need not instrument each call. (Addresses DR1, DR5.) Coverage differs across single calls, single-agent runs and crews, and a failed mint is not a verified record.

\textbf{DP2 (Retain content and bind required context).} Keep source content outside the receipt, and commit to the inputs, successful outputs and execution context that the review needs. Check retained bytes against those commitments, require the expected bindings, and mark absent material as incomplete. Reviewers can then inspect evidence independently without every receipt carrying full content. (Addresses DR2, DR4, DR5.) Section~\ref{sec:artifact} describes the optional fields and the treatment of failed outputs.

\textbf{DP3 (Deterministic routing with recorded control outcomes).} Choose providers and models as a deterministic function of the request, execution policy and capability catalog, and retain enough context to recompute the choice. Record configured control outcomes, and treat an output-contract violation as terminal, so that a run is never retried until a prohibited output passes. (Addresses DR3.) Recomputable routing is a property of execution. It neither explains the model's reasoning nor authorizes anyone to act on its output.

\textbf{DP4 (Minimize disclosure and verify before loading).} Redact configured fields before canonicalization and signing, and verify a receipt before the materialization path loads any artifact it references. Specified values then stay out of the signed bytes, and a forged receipt cannot trigger loading through that path. (Addresses DR1, DR2, DR4.) Detection has limited coverage, access to retained content needs separate controls, and unsalted hashes allow guessable values to be confirmed. Salted or keyed commitments would need their own design and evaluation.

Read across its rows, Table~\ref{tab:governance-trace} states the design knowledge in a form that does not depend on Lattice. Any runtime that issues evidence for model runs can be assessed against the same five requirements and the same tests. The boundary column lists claims that such evidence cannot carry on its own: issuer honesty, decision correctness, human authorization, privacy beyond the tested markers, and acceptable organizational cost. Lattice is one instantiation. The evidence reported for it demonstrates DR1 within the tested scope and supports DR2--DR5 only partially.

\section{Retrospective Review of Actual Refund Disputes}\label{sec:refund-case}

A refund dispute becomes an accountability problem when an organization must show what it told a customer, what its terms allowed, and what it later did about the money. Each question is answered by a different record, and the records are easy to conflate: an offer is not a credit, a temporary credit is not a refund, and an order to pay is not a payment. We use four published refund decisions to examine whether an AI-assisted review can keep these records apart and leave evidence that someone else can check.

\subsection{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada}

Moffatt v.\ Air Canada is the central case \citep{moffatt2024}. In November 2022, after their grandmother died, Jake Moffatt asked the chatbot on Air Canada's website about bereavement fares. The chatbot said that a ticket could be submitted for a reduced bereavement rate within 90 days of issue, even after travel. The Air Canada webpage it linked to said that the policy did not apply once travel was complete \citep[paras.~15--17]{moffatt2024}. Relying on the chatbot, Moffatt booked two one-way flights and applied within the stated window, but later learned from Air Canada employees that retroactive applications were not permitted. An Air Canada representative later acknowledged that the chatbot had used ``misleading words'' \citep[paras.~2, 18--22]{moffatt2024}. The Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation. It rejected the airline's suggestion that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements, holding that the chatbot was part of the website and that the airline was responsible for all information on it \citep[paras.~27--32]{moffatt2024}.

For this study, the decisive feature of the case is evidential. The customer's screenshot brought the chatbot's words into the proceeding \citep[para.~15]{moffatt2024}. Air Canada supplied a screenshot of part of its webpage but no information about the nature of its chatbot \citep[paras.~14, 16]{moffatt2024}. It relied on its tariff but did not provide the relevant portion, so its contractual argument failed \citep[para.~31]{moffatt2024}. It also claimed a CAD~200 goodwill coupon as a set-off, but provided no evidence that Moffatt had accepted the coupon \citep[para.~41]{moffatt2024}. The decision does not show whether the airline held better records; it shows that the airline did not produce them. An organization that cannot show what its automated channel said, and under which terms, is left with the customer's account of events. This is the kind of gap that run-level evidence is meant to narrow. Lattice played no part in this dispute, and nothing here shows that it would have changed the outcome.

The decision also marks the boundary between an order and a settlement. Paragraph~44 orders Air Canada to pay CAD~812.02 within 14 days: CAD~650.88 in damages, CAD~36.14 in pre-judgment interest and CAD~125 in tribunal fees. The total is therefore not a ticket refund alone. Paragraph~47 explains that a validated copy of the order can be enforced through the Provincial Court of British Columbia \citep[paras.~44--47]{moffatt2024}. Neither paragraph is evidence of payment, and a decision issued on 14 February 2024 cannot record what happened after that date. Proof of settlement would be a different record, such as a payment confirmation or the claimant's acknowledgment, and the published decision contains none. The review task therefore asks whether the decision establishes payment, and the rubric treats ``no settlement evidence'' as the correct answer.

Published records are not necessarily self-consistent either. According to the tribunal, Moffatt paid CAD~1,630.36 for the two flights, and damages are calculated from that figure \citep[paras.~34, 40]{moffatt2024}. However, the two fares it records sum to CAD~1,640.36 \citep[para.~18]{moffatt2024}. The record cannot tell a reviewer which figure matches the actual charges; it can only show that they disagree. A review packet that ties each extracted value to the page it came from makes the disagreement visible, whereas a bare answer would hide it.

\subsection{Three ombudsman decisions}

Three decisions of the United Kingdom Financial Ombudsman Service present the same distinctions in other forms. In DRN-4377241, a booking website had registered its own email address with the airline, so the customer could not complete the airline's verification step for a refund, and the website declined to help. The ombudsman directed the customer's bank to refund GBP~77.44 with interest. It also directed the bank to pay GBP~100 compensation and refund GBP~19.41 in interest and fees, in each case only if it had not already done so \citepalias{fos4377241}. DRN-4148727 separates three sums that a hurried summary could merge: an approximately GBP~126 chargeback refund to the customer, GBP~958 that the airline later paid to the travel agency, and GBP~150 that the bank paid for service failings. The complaint was not upheld \citepalias{fos4148727}. In DRN-3785437, the bank temporarily credited GBP~13 while pursuing a chargeback and reversed the credit when it stopped \citepalias{fos3785437}. Each decision asks the consumer to accept or reject it by a stated date, and none records what happened afterwards.

\subsection{The executed review}

\input{public-refund-case.tex}

Figure~\ref{fig:public-refund-workflow} shows the executed workflow. The application fingerprints each PDF, extracts its pages and gives every page an identifier. It passes all pages of one decision, a fixed review task and an execution policy to Lattice. The task asks five factual questions per decision, including whether the decision establishes payment. Expected answers and accepted pages are not part of the prompt. A pinned free model returns structured answers, each with the identifiers of its supporting pages, together with a short review note. Lattice checks the response format and signs a terminal receipt that commits to the task, policy, serialized request and output. Afterwards, a separate verifier process checks the signature, recomputes the hashes and confirms that every page appears in the recorded request. A frozen rubric scores values and page references. These checks answer different questions: a valid receipt can preserve a wrong answer, and a correct answer is unusable if it cannot be parsed. Table~\ref{tab:public-refund-evidence} lists what each step retains and where its evidence stops.

The review is retrospective. The models read decisions whose outcomes are stated in the text, so the task is careful extraction rather than prediction. The four records were chosen for their evidential features rather than sampled, are public and may appear in model training data, and are published accounts rather than the companies' own systems or exhibit files. No refund was approved or paid in this experiment, and no disputes reviewer used the resulting packets.

\paragraph{Receipt contents.} A signed receipt body holds the run identifier, issue time, signing key identifier, requested and observed model, selected route, token usage and cost, contract verdict and hash, input-artifact and output hashes, lineage root, and redaction manifest. It can also carry redacted tripwire evidence or route rejection reasons. Audience, nonce, expiry, parent, and closure fields remain optional. Schema v1.4 adds optional \code{taskHash}, \code{policyHash}, and \code{providerRequestHashes}. Current signed \code{ai.run} and public \code{ai.runAgent} results populate the task and effective-policy hashes using SHA-256 over canonical JSON. The ordered request list is included only when every executed adapter supplies the submission callback and at least one body was prepared; it includes prepared attempts that later fail. Each entry hashes the exact serialized UTF-8 body handed to configured fetch, after packaging. Transport headers and the endpoint URL are excluded from that hash, and raw content is not placed in the receipt. A custom fetch can subsequently modify those bytes, and the digest proves neither remote receipt nor model consumption. Legacy receipts and adapters without complete callback coverage remain visibly unbound. Verifiers accept optional fields when absent unless the caller supplies corresponding expected digests; a version number alone does not require their presence. Routing candidates, scores, and catalog version remain outside the signed record, so DR2 and DR3 require separately retained evidence beyond these commitments.

\paragraph{Provider coverage.} Seven adapters (OpenAI, generic OpenAI-compatible endpoints, Anthropic, Google Gemini, xAI, OpenRouter, and LM Studio) share a provider contract. Current ordinary and streaming builders deliver supported image values as native image parts, preserve JSON values, and reject unsupported or unsatisfied transports before submission. Binary image views retain their byte ranges; URL delivery remains subject to \code{noPublicUrl}. The earlier vision probe in Section~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} records the old builder, which dropped binary and object values or sent image data as text; it is historical evidence of the defect, not a measurement of the correction. Optional \code{generation.temperature} and \code{generation.maxOutputTokens} now flow through calls, agents, and crews into provider-specific wire fields. Native OpenAI uses \code{max\_completion\_tokens}, compatible gateways use \code{max\_tokens}, and native Anthropic and Gemini use their own fields. Omitted settings preserve earlier defaults. The request digest records these serialized choices, but does not validate provider capability claims or prove that a model used an input. The router's capability catalog remains a point-in-time registry supplemented by maintained profiles.

\paragraph{Agent runs.} Public \code{ai.runAgent} now returns a signed terminal receipt on successful and failed result variants when an intent or runtime signer is configured, independently of tracing. It commits to the original task, effective policy, terminal verdict, successful output when available, and the complete observed provider-submission sequence when supported. Its default signer-failure behavior returns \code{receiptError}; the configured throw mode raises an error. Per-iteration checkpoints remain a separate tracer facility. Crew execution retains its existing receipt accounting: a crew-start root, a completion receipt for the root and each successful child run, and a crew-close receipt. Thus $N$ agents that each run once with successful children yield $N+2$ crew receipts; a failed child has no crew completion receipt. The crew-start hash covers the nested identifiers, not the whole specification, and completion receipts hash outputs. The single-agent extension does not retroactively add full task, tool, or policy commitments to these crew records. Current \code{verifyChain} enforces the closing member list; historical 1.6.x verifiers do not. \code{requireClosure} rejects missing closure, while \code{partial} permits only listed subsets with a closer. Crew work time ends before root completion and closure signing, but includes start and child signing. Failed mints are reported under the signer-error policy; a verifiable closing list names only receipts actually minted, so callers must also inspect \code{receiptError}.

\paragraph{External executors.} An organization that already uses another orchestration framework can call an audit helper that mints the same receipt family from externally produced request, response, and output data. This offers an incremental integration path relevant to DR5, without measuring adoption effort, and requires trusting the external executor's account of the provider call.

\paragraph{Command-line tool.} The tool verifies receipts and receipt chains, prints structured advisories as warnings or information lines, checks stored artifacts and outputs offline against the signed hashes, compares receipts structurally, and gates regressions in cost and output consistency across a corpus of receipts in continuous integration. The library can also export run events through OpenTelemetry \citep{otel}, and tool results from the Model Context Protocol \citep{mcp} can be recorded as artifacts.

\subsection{Integrity under attack (DR1)}

We generated \TamperBase{} receipts from runs across all seven adapters and applied the mutations in Table~\ref{tab:tamper}. Of the cases the harness classifies as attacks, \TamperRejected{} of \TamperAttacks{} were rejected. Body edits failed signature verification whether the attacker kept the original signature, re-canonicalized correctly, or re-signed with an untrusted key under the legitimate key identifier. Non-canonical encodings were rejected as canonicalization mismatches. A valid receipt presented a second time is accepted: the verifier keeps no replay cache. Audience, nonce, and a caller-supplied version floor are checked only when requested. \code{expiresAt}, when the issuer set it, is enforced on every verification. Presenting the same receipt again is therefore not, by itself, a failed verification. What the verifier did not check is reported as structured advisories, each with a code and a severity. A warning means the receipt is bound to an audience or a nonce that the caller did not check. An information advisory means the receipt carries no audience, nonce, or expiry, or sits below the current schema version. The command-line verifier always prints warnings and prints information lines unless \code{--quiet} is set. Figure~\ref{fig:rw-tamper-heat} applies comparable mutation classes to the other record systems of Section~\ref{sec:realworld}.

Chain checks are separate from the single-receipt \code{VerifyErrorKind} taxonomy. \code{verifyChain} reports its own error kinds (\code{VerifyChainErrorKind}): an invalid link, no root, multiple roots, an orphan link, a run-identifier mismatch, a duplicate content identifier, a count mismatch, or a root mismatch. Reordering the receipt array is not an error, because the verifier follows parent links rather than array position. Dropping a middle link, splicing a foreign link, or replacing the root fails that check. Truncating the tail of an open chain does not. The result is success plus a tail-truncation warning unless the caller passes \code{expectedCount}. A chain whose root declares \code{chainClosure} fails with a count mismatch when a leaf or the closing receipt is missing, with no expected count, and a malformed closing receipt fails as an invalid link. \code{requireClosure} turns a missing closure declaration into a failure, and \code{partial} accepts a disclosed subset only when the closing receipt is present and lists every presented receipt, and warns when some listed members were not presented. Table~\ref{tab:v16} records these checks together with the advisories, crew receipts, and signer-failure modes. \VerifierChecksPassed{} of \VerifierChecks{} checks passed. Two of them run the released 1.6.0 verifier on a closed crew chain and confirm that it accepts the chain without enforcing closure.

\subsection{Verifiable retained evidence (DR2)}

For each of the seven adapters we recorded runs against provider-shaped fixtures, wrote the receipt and content-addressed artifacts to disk, reloaded them, verified the receipt, materialized a replay envelope, replayed offline, and recomputed the output hash. \ReplayVerified{} of \ReplayRuns{} runs verified, and \ReplayHash{} reproduced the signed output hash. Re-executing each run against the same fixture matched in \ReplayLive{} cases, and a one-character change to a recorded output was detected in \ReplayAltered{} cases (Table~\ref{tab:replay}). These results show that the record is internally consistent. Offline replay does not call a model: \code{replayOffline} returns the outputs the caller supplies, and the check is that they hash to the signed output hash. The result is therefore a signed output binding, which other signed record systems also provide (Table~\ref{tab:rw-matrix}). On the hosted rerun, every replayed receipt verified and a one-character edit was detected, and the signed output hash matched the kept answer except on trials that stored an empty answer after a provider failure (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-replay}). That mismatch is not a signature or chain failure. The check does not show that a live model would reproduce its output, and repeated live calls often returned different answers (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-drift}). Those historical bindings covered input artifacts and outputs only, with no task commitment. Current optional v1.4 fields extend that boundary, but do not turn the historical replay counts into tests of task or policy binding. In a crew chain, the crew-start receipt hashes the nested agent tree (\code{\{kind: "crew-start", agents: \{id, children\}\}}), each completion receipt hashes its agent's output, and the crew-close receipt hashes its member list.

\subsection{Inspectable execution and control outcomes (DR3)}\label{sec:eval-policy}

\paragraph{Routing.} Using a synthetic catalog of 40 model capabilities and \RouteRequests{} random requests with random policies, repeated decisions were identical at rate \RouteIdentical{}. An independently implemented oracle found \RouteViolations{} selected routes that violated provider allow or deny lists, privacy class, logging, latency, or image-modality constraints, and \RouteFalseNo{} cases in which the router reported no route although a feasible candidate existed. Reordering the catalog changed the selected model at a same-selection rate of \RoutePermSame{}. Catalog position is not part of the score, and ties break on provider and model identifiers. The receipt signs the selected route and the requested and observed model. In the historical experiment it did not sign the policy. Current task and policy hashes permit expected-content checks, while the policy itself, candidate list, scores, and catalog version still require separately retained records to recompute the decision.

\paragraph{Personal-data invariant.} The following rules and scores describe the detector revisions used by the historical experiments. They cover four types, and each rule trades recall for precision in a stated way. Email addresses match as written. A dashed United States social security number (SSN) matches as written. Spaced and undashed nine-digit forms match only when an SSN cue such as \code{ssn}, \code{ss\#}, or \code{social security} is the nearest word. Separated United States phone numbers match as written. A plain ten-digit run matches only when it is a valid North American number and the nearest word is a phone cue (\code{phone}, \code{tel}, \code{call}, \code{mobile}, \code{cell}, or \code{fax}) or a \code{+1} or \code{1-} trunk prefix precedes it. A payment card number is any Luhn-valid run of 13 to 19 digits, except runs that start with 0 or 1 and 13-digit runs that start with 978 or 979, which are ISBN-13 numbers. An SSN, phone, or card match is suppressed when an identifier label (\code{order}, \code{ord}, \code{invoice}, \code{inv}, \code{sku}, \code{ref}, \code{po}, \code{isbn}, \code{pn}, or \code{txn}) is the nearest token on the same line, joined to the number only by whitespace or by \code{:}, \code{\#}, \code{=}, \code{no.}, \code{number}, or \code{is}, unless the number is a phone number with a trunk prefix. Labels do not suppress email addresses. Words such as customer, account, and ticket are not labels. The trade-offs follow from these rules. A real SSN written as \code{order 123-45-6789} is not flagged. A bare nine- or ten-digit number without a type cue is not flagged, whether or not it is personal data. About one in ten random digit strings passes the Luhn check, so a text that lists $k$ numeric identifiers of 13 to 19 digits is flagged as holding a card with probability near $1-0.9^{k}$. The detectors see only strings, so a value stored under a key such as \code{ssn} is judged by its digits alone.

We built a labeled set of synthetic strings, 100 per label. In-scope positives cover each form the detectors claim, support-ticket text in which a number follows words such as customer, account holder, or patient ID, CSV and markdown rows, and card numbers from several networks. Out-of-scope personal data are names with addresses, international bank account numbers, United Kingdom phone numbers, IP addresses, dates of birth, and spelled-out email addresses. Negatives include order identifiers and stock-keeping units shaped like SSNs or phone numbers, 16-digit references, ISBN-13 numbers, and uncued numbers such as timestamps, byte counts, and bank routing numbers. On in-scope types plus all negatives, \code{noPII} reached precision \PiiInPrecision{} and recall \PiiInRecall{} (\(n=\PiiInN\), false positives \PiiInFp{}, false negatives \PiiInFn{}). The false negatives come from forms the rules leave out by design, such as a plain ten-digit number after ``call the customer at'' and an undashed SSN in a CSV row with no SSN cue. The \PiiNewNegN{} uncued numeric negatives produced \PiiNewNegFp{} false positives. Counting every personal-data label, including types the detectors do not claim, precision was \PiiAllPrecision{} and recall \PiiAllRecall{} (Table~\ref{tab:policy}, Figure~\ref{fig:pii}). The cases were chosen with the rules in view, and the identifier-shaped negatives carry the labels the rules key on, so these figures show that the rules behave as specified. They are not prevalence-weighted estimates and not estimates of precision on real text. On public datasets, SSN and phone precision and recall were far lower, because passport numbers and other identifiers share the same digit shapes (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}, Figure~\ref{fig:rw-pii}, Tables~\ref{tab:rw-pii-ai4} and~\ref{tab:rw-pii-gretel}, Figures~\ref{fig:rw-pii-f1} and~\ref{fig:rw-pii-latency}). Those public-data rows were measured with the rules at commit \texttt{a8cc091}, which differ from the rules above as Section~\ref{sec:realworld} describes.

\paragraph{Citation invariant.} The \code{mustCite} invariant checks that an output cites a named artifact. By default it reads only structured lists: a top-level \code{citations} or \code{evidence} list whose string entries, or whose objects' \code{source} field, name the artifact. Matching is case-sensitive unless case folding is requested, and other object keys can be named. If the top-level list is missing or omits the artifact, nested \code{citations} and \code{evidence} lists are searched, unless the contract declares a path. The default never reads prose. Three options extend it, and each is added to the contract only when set, so the hash of an existing contract does not change. With \code{inline}, a bracketed or parenthesized group in the prose whose content, or one comma- or semicolon-separated part of it, equals the artifact name also counts; it does not override a non-empty structured list that omits the artifact. With \code{rejectUncited}, a matching list entry is rejected when the prose contains citation-like square brackets, such as \code{[1]}, \code{[1-3]}, or \code{[Smith 1999]}, and none of them resolves to the artifact. Links, footnote markers, and code are not treated as citations. \code{textPath} limits these prose checks to one field. The invariant agreed with the labels in \MustCiteAgree{} of \MustCiteCases{} constructed cases: \MustCiteDefaultAgree{} of \MustCiteDefaultCases{} for the default and \MustCiteOptInAgree{} of \MustCiteOptInCases{} for the options. The cases include numeric markers, links, and path-scoped outputs, and they were also chosen with the implementation in view. ASQA long answers are prose without citation lists, so the default accepts a gold title in none of them. The ASQA count in Section~\ref{sec:realworld} was measured at commit \texttt{a8cc091}, where an inline marker containing the name counted by default. The check is syntactic. It does not judge whether the cited artifact supports the claim \citep{lewis2020rag}.

\subsection{Minimized disclosure (DR4)}\label{sec:eval-min}

We placed marker secrets in the task, an input artifact, the provider credential, and the model output, then scanned the decoded signed payload, with and without a \code{noPII} invariant. Marker hits in signed bytes were \LeakHits{} of \LeakRows{} rows (Table~\ref{tab:policy} does not repeat the per-adapter log; the raw rows are in \code{eval/results/policy.json}). Receipts carry hashes of the input artifacts and the output. In that historical recording, neither the task text nor the credential appeared in the signed bytes, in clear or as a hash. Current receipts add an unsalted task digest and may hash request bodies containing sensitive content; credentials in transport headers are outside that digest. When the invariant fires, the in-process tripwire result still includes the matched substring so the caller can act on it. The value that is signed is a detector name, a length, and a mask, so the signed bytes hold the masked form that the redaction manifest describes. The scan checks for the marker strings themselves. It does not test whether a hash can be inverted: a low-entropy input or output, such as an SSN or a yes/no answer, can be confirmed from its unsalted hash by dictionary search (Section~\ref{sec:requirements}, DP4). Among the record systems compared in Section~\ref{sec:realworld}, Figure~\ref{fig:rw-bytes} shows which kept the plaintext transcript and which kept only digests or ciphertext when wrapping the same recorded request and response bytes.

\subsection{Operational and adoption costs (DR5)}

Table~\ref{tab:overhead} and Figure~\ref{fig:overhead} report latency over \OverheadTrials{} trials after warm-up, against a mocked provider. Signing added \SignDeltaSmallMs{}~ms at the median for a 1~KB text input and \SignDeltaImageMs{}~ms for a 1~MB image (signed run minus unsigned run). Verification of the small-text receipt was \VerifySmallMs{}~ms at the median. The receipt grows with the number of input hashes rather than with payload size (Table~\ref{tab:size}). Linked receipts and chain verification scale with depth (Table~\ref{tab:chain}, Figure~\ref{fig:chain}). Because the provider was a zero-latency fake, these figures are the runtime's own cost. Integration effort, retention administration, and reviewer effort were not measured. No organizational acceptance budget was fixed for these historical results; free API spend is not evidence of low adoption cost.

\subsection{Retrospective public-record review}\label{sec:eval-public-refund}

Table~\ref{tab:public-refund-results} reports the 16 reviews of Section~\ref{sec:refund-case}: four decisions, two models and two repetitions. Nemotron returned usable answers in all eight runs and passed every value and page check. Dots returned seven usable responses. Every Moffatt review answered that the decision does not establish payment of the CAD~812.02 order, and every DRN-4377241 review kept the directed refund, fees and compensation apart.

The rubric needed correcting in both directions. A first audit found that DRN-4148727 states its outcome on page~1 as well as page~2, which would raise the full-rubric score to 15/16. Applying the same standard to every graded answer and page also showed that the rubric had accepted Moffatt page~4 as support for the CAD~1,630.36 total. That page records only the two fares, which sum to CAD~1,640.36, and one Dots review had cited page~4 alone. With both corrections the result remains 14/16. We report the frozen and fully audited results together rather than the more favorable partial correction. One Dots review note flagged the CAD~10 discrepancy, although no question asked about it. The rubric missed the discrepancy, and review notes are not graded.

All 16 terminal signatures verified, including that of the failed run. Fifteen runs carried successful-output commitments and passed full local verification. The failed run has no output commitment, so its raw response is retained but not counted as committed evidence. These results show that the evidence trail can be produced and checked on real dispute records using free models, with no added account spend. They do not show that a disputes reviewer would reach better or faster decisions with it.

\subsection{Planned naturalistic evaluation with developers and auditors}\label{sec:eval-study}

The artificial evaluation establishes technical efficacy but not utility for the people who must act on the evidence. Following FEDS \citep{venable2016}, we plan a naturalistic episode. \todo{Lakshman and Prof.\ Neouchi: approve, adapt, or replace the following design, and obtain UT Dallas IRB determination before recruiting.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Participants:} 12--20 participants in two roles, software developers who build LLM features and auditors or compliance analysts.
\item \emph{Tasks:} (a) developers instrument an existing LLM feature with receipts, measuring time to adoption and code changes (DR5); (b) auditors receive a set of runs, some tampered and some with policy violations, as either conventional logs or receipts with the command-line tool, and must identify which runs are trustworthy and which violated policy (DR1, DR3).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Contribution to IS knowledge}

In terms of the knowledge contribution framework of \citet{gregor2013}, this study offers an improvement: a new solution to a known problem, auditable record keeping, for a new application context. Its contribution has two levels. At the level of nascent design theory \citep{gregor2007}, it contributes five requirements and four principles for run-level AI accountability that apply beyond the specific artifact. At the level of the instantiation, it contributes an open-source artifact and a reusable evaluation harness, together with quantitative evidence about which principles the instantiation realizes. The evaluation also yields design knowledge that is easy to overlook. A valid signature under a trusted key protects committed content against undetected modification under the stated cryptographic assumptions. It does not establish freshness; expiry, audience, nonce, and maximum-age checks depend on the available fields and verifier expectations. A receipt attests only to what is hashed into it. Current task and policy commitments let a verifier check retained inputs against the signed digests; absence or unchecked expectations still leave that question unanswered. Historical dropped-image failures show why artifact hashes alone are insufficient. The new submission digest narrows the boundary to bytes handed to configured fetch, while remote receipt and consumption remain unproven. A hash hides a value only when the value is hard to guess. Linking receipts does not by itself reveal a missing tail. A closing receipt that lists members does, but only for verifiers that enforce it. A redaction manifest is a claim unless the signed bytes are the masked form, which the leakage scan checks. Offline replay of stored outputs is an output binding, not a re-execution, and other signed record systems offer the same binding (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-capability}). These boundaries inform proposed designs for organizational audit trails; transfer and usefulness require further evaluation.

The study also connects IS research on AI governance \citep{berente2021,mantymaki2022,papagiannidis2025} with the security literature on tamper-evident records \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009,laurie2013}. Governance research has described \emph{what} organizations must be able to show. This study demonstrates \emph{how} an information system can be designed so that the evidence is produced as a by-product of normal operation rather than as a separate compliance activity, extending the idea of sociotechnical envelopment \citep{asatiani2021} to the evidentiary layer.

\subsection{Implications for practice}

For managers and compliance officers, the historical measurements suggest that run-level evidence can add small processing costs on the tested workloads. New binding and transport paths require their own measurements before that conclusion is generalized. Evidence of this kind is relevant to conditional high-risk record-keeping provisions in the EU AI Act \citep[Arts.~12, 19, 26]{euai2026text}, to selected outcomes of the voluntary NIST AI RMF \citep{nist2023}, and to the documented controls an ISO/IEC 42001 management system expects \citep{iso42001}. It can supply concrete artifacts for the application layer of LLM audits \citep{mokander2023}. Three cautions follow from the evaluation. First, successful verification establishes integrity of the recorded commitments under the trusted key, not issuer honesty or legal compliance. Organizations that need protection against a dishonest operator should combine receipts with receiver-side attestations or transparency logs, as in Sello \citep{figuera2026sello} or Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013}. Second, auditors should retain task, effective policy, request bodies, and routing-catalog evidence separately, require the relevant optional commitments, and supply expected digests during verification. Chain validity must be read alongside any reported receipt-mint failure. Third, pattern-based personal-data checks are not a complete privacy control. On the synthetic set they behave as specified, but on public datasets their SSN and phone precision and recall were low (Tables~\ref{tab:rw-pii-ai4} and~\ref{tab:rw-pii-gretel}), because other identifiers share the same digit shapes. The historical rules also missed types they did not claim and some labeled or uncued numbers; later contextual rules require their own evaluation.

For developers, the design principles suggest a pattern: configure signing once, commit to hashes rather than content, make provider-routing decisions deterministic and retain their context, and verify before loading.

\subsection{Limitations and future research}

The evaluation has several limitations. Some of them were first reported in an evaluation filed as a public issue \citep{latticeissue21}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Historical mocked and free-hosted samples.} The original controlled tables use a simulated provider. Sections~\ref{sec:realworld} and~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} record a local model and free hosted models. That hosted sample is a free tier: it rate-limits, it does not represent paid latency, and one catalogued model rejects ordinary chat calls. A rerun at commit \texttt{c5f79b0} on the author's machine, started 2026-10-26 14:13 CDT, measured median Lattice processing of 1.7 to 3.0~ms (0.07 to 0.27\% of end-to-end; Liquid's p95 was 0.72\%), which is higher than the previous free-tier run. Two crews stopped at the wall-clock budget after free-tier waits and are not successes. The single-agent stage was not included in that rerun. The adapter at that historical revision sent no temperature or token cap, so its fetch wrapper added both. The current API forwards explicit generation settings before hashing the submitted body; the old timing results do not measure that path. Provider-time differences between a packed prompt and a one-line prompt are too noisy to attribute to the extra tokens.
\item \emph{Optional bindings and retained evidence.} Current v1.4 receipts support task, policy, and ordered request digests; legacy receipts and unsupported adapters can lack them. A verifier must supply expected commitments when their absence or mismatch should fail. Raw task, policy, prompt, routing candidates, and catalog evidence are not reconstructed from the receipt, and routing rationale remains outside its commitments.
\item \emph{Terminal evidence is not a complete event log.} Public \code{ai.runAgent} now returns a terminal receipt without requiring a tracer. Per-iteration checkpoints remain separate, and crew receipts retain their existing tree/accounting format. A terminal digest does not establish that every tool effect was independently witnessed or durably recorded before execution.
\item \emph{Submission is not consumption.} Current builders send supported images natively, preserve JSON, and fail unsupported transports. The earlier vision probe in Section~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} misread a red image sent as text; it was not repeated at commit \texttt{c5f79b0} and remains evidence about that earlier builder. Correct byte packaging alone does not establish image comprehension or remote consumption. The callback boundary excludes subsequent custom-fetch mutation, transport headers, and receiver attestation.
\item \emph{Unsalted hashes.} Artifact, output, task, policy, and request commitments use unsalted SHA-256. A holder who can guess the committed content can confirm it, including low-entropy decisions or inputs; these digests are not encryption.
\item \emph{Freshness binding is opt-in.} Audience, nonce, and a raised version floor are not required for verification. A receipt with no \code{expiresAt} does not expire unless the caller passes \code{maxAgeMs}. There is no replay cache. Advisories report unchecked bindings as warnings or information. They do not reject.
\item \emph{Open chains can still be tail-truncated.} Without \code{expectedCount} or a closing member list, truncation produces a warning, not a failure. Crew chains carry a closing member list, but verifiers in the 1.6.x line do not enforce it.
\item \emph{Signer failure is visible, not fail-closed by default.} If the signer throws, the run still returns its result, which carries \code{receiptError}, unless \code{onSignerError} is set to throw. A crew-start signing failure always throws, because no agent work has run. A run with no signer emits no receipt.
\item \emph{Pattern-based personal-data checks.} Historical detector measurements covered email, United States SSNs, payment cards, and phone patterns. Labels, type cues, and long numeric identifiers produced the documented misses and false positives. The public-data rows in Section~\ref{sec:realworld} were remeasured at commit \texttt{ae99fb5}; their SSN/phone trade-offs and card false positives are retained as historical findings. Current all-match and optional contextual handling require separate held-out scoring. Neither blocking a sample nor matching a marker establishes complete privacy protection or correct entity-type classification.
\item \emph{must-cite is syntactic.} By default it reads only structured citation lists. Prose checks are opt-in. It does not decide whether a cited artifact supports the prose. On ASQA dev, that default accepts a gold title in 0 of 948 answers. With \texttt{inline:~true} it accepts 4. An earlier count of 7 used a default that read inline markers.
\item \emph{Self-attestation and synthetic data.} Receipts are signed by the issuing runtime. The policy evaluation used synthetic data, and its labeled cases were chosen with the rules they test in view.
\item \emph{No human evaluation yet} (Sect.~\ref{sec:eval-study}).
\end{itemize}
Future research can replace unsalted hashes with salted or keyed commitments, bind more of the routing catalog and tool-effect history, combine issuer receipts with receiver attestations and transparency logs, study how auditors use receipts in practice, and examine their role in conformity assessments. New commitments and corrected transport should be evaluated on their own revisions rather than credited with historical results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Organizations need evidence with which responsible people can review and justify AI-assisted decisions. The public refund-dispute records make that need concrete. In Moffatt v.\ Air Canada, the customer's screenshot, not the airline's own records, established what its chatbot had said, and the airline did not produce the contract terms it relied on. This study addresses the run-level evidence needed within that broader organizational review process. It develops five design requirements and four principles and instantiates them in Lattice.

The historical controlled evaluation provides evidence about technical behavior: where rejection was required, single-receipt tamper attempts were rejected, retained outputs matched signed hashes, routing policies were not violated, and signing added milliseconds per run (Table~\ref{tab:eval-map}). Separate hosted cohorts examine execution and answer quality. Later task, policy, and serialized-request commitments address identified gaps, but historical timings do not measure those extensions and request commitments do not prove remote consumption. Checking a stored output against its signed hash is verification of retained evidence, not re-execution of the model.

These results support a design for producing verifiable evidence during execution. They do not establish decision correctness, fairness, provider honesty, human authorization, or regulatory compliance. Self-attestation, unsalted hashes, optional verifier expectations, and incomplete external evidence remain substantive limits. Organizations must combine technical verification with retained business records, assigned review authority, and judgment about the decision itself. Whether this design helps managers investigate disputes or improves audit work remains a question for practitioner evaluation; no such benefit is claimed from the reported experiments.
