\begin{abstract}
\noindent Organizations embed large language models in business processes, yet auditors need evidence of what a system was asked, how it was configured, and what it produced. This design-science study states five requirements and four principles for run-level accountability and instantiates them in Lattice, an open-source runtime. When a signer is configured, a run emits a canonical receipt that is redacted, signed, and replayable. An offline evaluation on mocked providers rejects tampering of single receipts, replays recorded runs to the signed output hash, and adds milliseconds of signing cost. Replay binding, a version floor, and open-chain length checks stay verifier opt-ins. New crew runs close the receipt chain. Personal data matched by a privacy check is masked before signing. The study contributes design knowledge for accountable AI information systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from experimentation into operational information systems that approve refunds, triage claims, summarize case files, and draft regulated communications \citep{berente2021,bommasani2021}. These systems combine text, images, documents, and tool calls, and they often route a single task across several model providers for reasons of cost, latency, privacy, or capability \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm}. Their outputs therefore carry organizational consequences, and with consequences comes accountability: someone must be able to explain, after the fact, what the system was asked to do, which model served the request under which constraints, and what came back \citep{wieringa2020,novelli2023}.

Regulatory and standards frameworks now make this expectation explicit. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act requires providers of high-risk systems to enable automatic recording of events over the system's lifetime and to keep such logs \citep{euaiact2024}. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework asks organizations to document and monitor AI systems so that risks can be measured and managed \citep{nist2023}, and ISO/IEC 42001 specifies a management system in which AI-related processes must be controlled and evidenced \citep{iso42001}. Scholarship on AI auditing reaches a similar conclusion: internal and external audits depend on artifacts that make claims about a system verifiable rather than merely asserted \citep{raji2020,brundage2020,mokander2021,mokander2023}.

Current practice falls short of this. Most organizations integrate models through provider wrappers and orchestration libraries that normalize request formats and add conveniences such as tool calling and tracing \citep{amershi2019,sculley2015}. Their traces are written for human debugging. A trace can be edited after the fact, it does not bind inputs to outputs, and a third party cannot check it without trusting the party that produced it \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009}. The result is an accountability gap between what governance frameworks expect and what AI-enabled information systems actually record \citep{raji2020,papagiannidis2025}.

This study addresses that gap through design science research (DSR) \citep{hevner2004,peffers2007,gregor2013}. The research question is: \emph{How should a model-run infrastructure be designed so that every run of an AI-enabled information system yields evidence that auditors can verify independently and operators can reproduce, at acceptable cost?} We derive design requirements from the governance and auditing literature, formulate design principles, instantiate them in an open-source artifact called Lattice \citep{lattice}, and evaluate the artifact against each requirement with a reproducible, offline evaluation harness. The central construct is the \emph{capability receipt}: a per-run record that is canonicalized, redacted before signing, signed with a public-key signature, and linked to content-addressed artifacts so that it can be verified and replayed offline.

The study makes three contributions. First, it specifies requirements and principles for run-level AI accountability, a nascent design theory in the sense of \citet{gregor2007} that is independent of any particular implementation. Second, it provides an instantiation whose behavior is evaluated quantitatively, covering integrity under attack, replay fidelity, policy enforcement, and runtime overhead. Third, it reports where the instantiation does \emph{not} meet its requirements, findings that are themselves design knowledge for builders of accountable AI systems \citep{baskerville2018}. We position the contribution as an \emph{improvement} in the sense of \citet{gregor2013}: a known problem, auditable record keeping, addressed with a new solution for a new class of systems, multi-provider model runs.

The remainder of the paper follows the DSR publication schema proposed by \citet{gregor2013}. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the research approach. Section~\ref{sec:requirements} derives requirements and principles. Section~\ref{sec:artifact} presents the artifact. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses contributions, implications, and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AI accountability, governance, and auditing}

Accountability in AI denotes the obligation of an actor to explain and justify the conduct of a system to a forum that can pass judgment and impose consequences \citep{wieringa2020,novelli2023}. IS research frames AI management as a matter of autonomy, learning, and inscrutability \citep{berente2021}, and it describes how organizations envelop AI with sociotechnical controls to contain its behavior \citep{asatiani2021}. Organizational AI governance comprises the structures, processes, and procedures that align AI use with organizational and regulatory goals \citep{mantymaki2022,schneider2022,papagiannidis2025}. Across these accounts, governance depends on evidence. Policies and controls mean little unless an organization can show, for specific decisions, that the controls operated.

The AI auditing literature makes this dependence concrete. \citet{raji2020} propose an end-to-end internal audit framework and stress documentation artifacts throughout the development life cycle. \citet{mokander2021} argue for ethics-based auditing as a governance mechanism, and \citet{mokander2023} propose a three-layered approach to auditing large language models at the governance, model, and application layers. \citet{brundage2020} call for mechanisms, including audit trails, that make claims about AI development verifiable by outsiders. Transparency research cautions that transparency must be designed for specific audiences and purposes \citep{felzmann2019,rai2020}, and human-centered AI calls for reliable, safe, and trustworthy systems backed by audit trails \citep{shneiderman2020}. Documentation instruments such as model cards \citep{mitchell2019} and datasheets for datasets \citep{gebru2021} standardize disclosure about models and data. However, they describe artifacts at release time, not individual production runs. The application layer of \citet{mokander2023}, where a model is used in a specific business process, is where the evidence gap is widest, and it is the layer this study targets.

\subsection{Tamper-evident logging, provenance, and transparency}

Computer security offers a long tradition of tamper-evident records. \citet{haber1991} showed how to time-stamp digital documents, and \citet{merkle1988} introduced hash-tree commitments. \citet{schneier1999} designed secure audit logs whose entries cannot be undetectably altered after compromise, and \citet{crosby2009} gave efficient data structures for tamper-evident logs with logarithmic proofs. Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013} publishes certificates to append-only logs so that misissuance becomes publicly detectable. Software supply chain research applies these ideas to builds. in-toto \citep{torresarias2019} signs each step of a software supply chain, The Update Framework addresses survivable key compromise \citep{samuel2010}, Sigstore makes signing and transparency logging accessible to developers \citep{newman2022}, and SLSA defines levels of supply chain integrity \citep{slsa}. The Dead Simple Signing Envelope (DSSE) specifies a minimal signed envelope with a pre-authentication encoding \citep{dsse}. Data provenance research models the derivation history of data products \citep{moreau2011,herschel2017}.

These lines of work supply mechanisms (canonical encodings, signatures, hash chains, transparency logs) but were not designed for the semantics of a model run. A model run has a declared intent, a routing decision among providers, policy constraints, output invariants, and multimodal inputs that may contain personal data.

\subsection{Verifiable inference and model routing}

A complementary stream seeks cryptographic guarantees that a specific model computed a specific output, using zero-knowledge proofs \citep{kang2022zk,sun2024}. Such proofs give strong guarantees but currently require access to model weights and impose high costs, so they do not apply to proprietary hosted models consumed through application programming interfaces (APIs). Work on model routing optimizes cost and quality by selecting among models \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm}, often with learned routers whose decisions are hard to audit. Security research on LLM-integrated applications shows that model outputs can be manipulated through indirect prompt injection \citep{greshake2023}, which raises the stakes for run-level evidence.

\subsection{Agent receipts and verifiable agent provenance}

Three recent proposals are closest to this study. \emph{TrustTrack} is a vision paper that argues for verifiable identity, policy commitments, and tamper-resistant behavioral logs in multi-agent systems \citep{li2025trusttrack}. \emph{Sello} (``Notarized Agents'') inverts the trust boundary: the service that receives an agent's call signs a receipt, encrypts it to the agent's owner, and publishes it to a witness-cosigned Merkle log, so that the owner need not trust the agent or its operator \citep{figuera2026sello}. \emph{Janus} places a signed, hash-chained log on the effect path of agentic LLMs, so that a step's proposal and verdict are durable before its effect is released. Its gates are pure functions of the log and can be re-derived offline, and it is evaluated under crash injection and with a lending workflow \citep{arslan2026janus}.

Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares these systems with the artifact presented here. The comparison shows that the contribution of this study is not the idea of signed records for AI, which all four share, but a specific combination of design features aimed at \emph{organizational} model use: issuer-side receipts emitted for every run that has a signer, a recorded and deterministic routing decision across heterogeneous providers, redaction before signing, and offline replay. The comparison also shows what Lattice lacks: Sello's receiver-side attestation and transparency log, and Janus's effect gating and crash-consistency evaluation. Lattice was designed independently. Its public repository dates from 21 April 2026 \citep{lattice}, before the Sello (June 2026) and Janus (September 2026) preprints appeared.

\section{Research Approach}\label{sec:method}

We follow the design science research methodology (DSRM) of \citet{peffers2007}, guided by the DSR guidelines of \citet{hevner2004} and the contribution framework of \citet{gregor2013}. Table~\ref{tab:dsrm} maps the six DSRM activities to this study. The entry point is problem-centered: the accountability gap arose while building a browser-automation product whose autonomous actions required an auditable record, and it generalizes to any organization that embeds models in business processes. DSR builds and evaluates artifacts to solve organizational problems while producing prescriptive knowledge \citep{vombrocke2020}, and it has long been linked to information systems design theories \citep{walls1992}. We use the anatomy of a design theory \citep{gregor2007} to structure the knowledge contribution, with requirements as meta-requirements, principles as principles of form and function, and Lattice as an expository instantiation. Evaluation follows the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS) of \citet{venable2016}. We adopt a technical risk and efficacy strategy, starting with artificial, summative evaluation of the instantiation against its requirements, and plan a naturalistic evaluation with practitioners as the next episode (Section~\ref{sec:eval-study}). Evaluation criteria are drawn from the taxonomy of \citet{prat2015} (goal attainment, efficiency, robustness, and consistency) and from the build-evaluate pattern of \citet{sonnenberg2012}.

\section{Design Requirements and Principles}\label{sec:requirements}

\subsection{Design requirements}

We derive five design requirements (DRs) from the governance, auditing, and secure logging literature. Each is stated at the level of the class of systems rather than of any particular implementation.

\textbf{DR1 (Integrity and attribution).} The record of each model run must be tamper-evident and attributable to an accountable issuer, so that any alteration is detectable by a party holding only public verification material. This follows from logging duties for high-risk systems \citep{euaiact2024}, from the verifiable-claims agenda \citep{brundage2020}, and from secure audit log research \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009}.

\textbf{DR2 (Reproducibility).} An auditor must be able to reconstruct a recorded run offline and confirm that the recorded inputs and outputs are those committed in the record, without access to the original system or provider. This reflects the role of reproducibility in audits \citep{raji2020} and in ML practice more broadly \citep{kapoor2023}.

\textbf{DR3 (Inspectable decisions and enforced policy).} The record must state which provider and model served the run and why, and declared policies (for example, privacy class, provider restrictions, budget, or citation and personal-data constraints) must be enforced, with violations recorded rather than silently retried. This reflects the inscrutability concern in AI management \citep{berente2021}, the need for explanations tailored to auditors \citep{felzmann2019}, and risk-management functions in \citet{nist2023}.

\textbf{DR4 (Data minimization).} The record must not disclose secrets or personal data beyond what accountability requires, so that it can be shared with auditors without creating a new privacy risk. This reflects data protection obligations and the transparency--privacy tension discussed by \citet{felzmann2019}.

\textbf{DR5 (Low adoption cost).} Producing the record must add negligible runtime overhead and require little change to existing application code, because accountability mechanisms that impose high costs are bypassed in practice \citep{sculley2015,amershi2019}.

\subsection{Design principles}

We formulate four design principles (DPs), following the convention that a principle names the action, the mechanism, and the rationale \citep{gregor2013}.

\textbf{DP1 (Receipt by default).} Provide the runtime with a signing key so that \emph{every} run, whether successful or failed, emits a receipt that is canonicalized, signed, and verifiable with a public key, because accountability evidence that depends on developer discipline is incomplete. (Addresses DR1, DR5.)

\textbf{DP2 (Commit to content, carry hashes).} Have the receipt commit to content-addressed hashes of inputs and outputs rather than to the content itself, and keep the content in a separate artifact store, because this makes receipts small and shareable while still allowing replay. (Addresses DR2, DR4, DR5.)

\textbf{DP3 (Decide deterministically and record the decision).} Implement provider and model selection as a pure function of a capability catalog, the request, and the policy, and record the selected route in the receipt, because a decision that can be recomputed can be audited. Treat output-policy violations as terminal, so that a run is never retried until a banned output passes. (Addresses DR3.)

\textbf{DP4 (Redact before signing, verify before loading).} Apply redaction before-canonicalization and signing, so that sensitive values never enter the signed bytes, and verify a receipt before loading any referenced artifact, so that a forged receipt cannot trigger side effects. (Addresses DR1, DR2, DR4.)

\paragraph{Provider coverage.} Seven provider adapters (OpenAI, generic OpenAI-compatible endpoints, Anthropic, Google Gemini, xAI, OpenRouter, and LM Studio for local models) implement a common contract so that a receipt has the same meaning regardless of which provider served the run. The router consults a capability catalog generated from a public model registry, supplemented by hand-maintained profiles.

\paragraph{Multi-agent runs.} For agent crews, each child receipt can carry the content identifier of its parent receipt. The crew-start receipt hashes the crew plan rather than leaving the output hash empty. Completion receipts hash each agent's output. A closing receipt lists the member content identifiers, so a dropped leaf fails chain verification.

\paragraph{External executors.} An organization that already uses another orchestration framework can call an audit helper that mints the same receipt family from externally produced request, response, and output data. This supports incremental adoption (DR5), at the price of trusting the external executor's account of the provider call.

\paragraph{Command-line tool.} The tool verifies receipts, replays them offline against stored artifacts, compares receipts structurally, and gates regressions in cost and output consistency across a corpus of receipts in continuous integration. It can also export run events through OpenTelemetry \citep{otel}, and tool results from the Model Context Protocol \citep{mcp} can be recorded as artifacts.

\subsection{Integrity under attack (DR1)}

We generated \TamperBase{} receipts from runs across all seven adapters and applied the mutations in Table~\ref{tab:tamper}. Of the cases the harness classifies as attacks, \TamperRejected{} of \TamperAttacks{} were rejected. Body edits failed signature verification whether the attacker kept the original signature, re-canonicalized correctly, or re-signed with an untrusted key under the legitimate key identifier. Non-canonical encodings were rejected as canonicalization mismatches. A valid receipt presented a second time is accepted: the verifier keeps no replay cache. Audience, nonce, and a caller-supplied version floor are checked only when requested. \code{expiresAt}, when the issuer set it, is enforced on every verification. Presenting the same receipt again is therefore not, by itself, a failed verification.

Chain checks are separate from the ten \code{VerifyErrorKind} values. \code{verifyChain} returns \code{VerifyChainErrorKind}: an invalid link, no root, multiple roots, an orphan link, a run-identifier mismatch, a duplicate content identifier, a count mismatch, or a root mismatch. Reordering the receipt array is not an error, because the verifier follows parent links rather than array position. Dropping a middle link, splicing a foreign link, or replacing the root fails that check. Truncating the tail of an open chain does not. The result is success plus an advisory unless the caller passes \code{expectedCount}. A crew chain that declares \code{chainClosure} and lists member content identifiers on a closing receipt fails closed when a leaf is missing, with no expected count. Table~\ref{tab:v16} records those opt-in checks.

\subsection{Reproducibility (DR2)}

For each of the seven adapters we recorded runs against provider-shaped fixtures, wrote the receipt and content-addressed artifacts to disk, reloaded them, verified the receipt, materialized a replay envelope, replayed offline, and recomputed the output hash. \ReplayVerified{} of \ReplayRuns{} runs verified, and \ReplayHash{} reproduced the signed output hash. Re-executing each run against the same fixture matched in \ReplayLive{} cases, and a one-character change to a recorded output was detected in \ReplayAltered{} cases (Table~\ref{tab:replay}). These results show that the record is internally consistent. They do not show that a live model would reproduce its output. The crew-start receipt now hashes the plan (\code{\{kind: crew-start, rootId, childIds\}}). Completion receipts hash agent output. The closing receipt hashes the member list.

\subsection{Inspectable decisions and policy enforcement (DR3)}\label{sec:eval-policy}

\paragraph{Routing.} Using a synthetic catalog of 40 model capabilities and \RouteRequests{} random requests with random policies, repeated decisions were identical at rate \RouteIdentical{}. An independently implemented oracle found \RouteViolations{} selected routes that violated provider allow or deny lists, privacy class, logging, latency, or image-modality constraints, and \RouteFalseNo{} cases in which the router reported no route although a feasible candidate existed. Reordering the catalog changed the selected model at a same-selection rate of \RoutePermSame{}. Catalog position is not part of the score. Ties break on provider and model identifiers, which are recorded with the decision. Auditors still need the catalog version, because the candidates themselves come from that catalog.

\paragraph{Output invariants.} We built a labeled set of synthetic strings containing in-scope personal data (email addresses, United States social security numbers in dashed, spaced, and undashed forms, payment card numbers, and United States phone numbers including a plain 10-digit form), out-of-scope personal data (names with addresses, international bank account numbers, IP addresses, dates of birth), and deliberately difficult negatives such as order identifiers and stock-keeping units shaped like social security or phone numbers, and 16-digit references. On in-scope types plus those negatives, \code{noPII} reached precision \PiiInPrecision{} and recall \PiiInRecall{} (\(n=\PiiInN\), false positives \PiiInFp{}, false negatives \PiiInFn{}). Counting every personal-data label, including types the detectors do not claim, precision was \PiiAllPrecision{} and recall \PiiAllRecall{} (Table~\ref{tab:policy}, Figure~\ref{fig:pii}). A dashed or undashed social security number is skipped when an identifier cue such as order, invoice, sku, or ref is among the two tokens before the number. That rule is what keeps order identifiers and phone-shaped SKUs from becoming false positives, and it is also why a spaced social security number can be missed when random prose happens to contain such a cue. Because the negatives were chosen adversarially, these figures are not prevalence-weighted estimates.

The \code{mustCite} invariant agreed with the labels in \MustCiteAgree{} of \MustCiteCases{} constructed cases. An inline \code{[name]} or \code{(name)} marker counts, as does a nested citations array. A citations-list entry that names the artifact is rejected when the prose's square-bracket citations name a different work. Default matching is case-sensitive and reads a string or a \code{source} field. Case folding and other object keys are explicit options, and the labeled set treats the default as a miss. A citations array that names the artifact, with no contradictory bracket in the prose, is accepted. The check does not judge whether the cited artifact supports the claim \citep{lewis2020rag}.

\subsection{Data minimization (DR4)}

We placed marker secrets in the task, an input artifact, the provider credential, and the model output, then scanned the decoded signed payload, with and without a \code{noPII} invariant. Marker hits in signed bytes were \LeakHits{} of \LeakRows{} rows (Table~\ref{tab:policy} does not repeat the per-adapter log; the raw rows are in \code{eval/results/policy.json}). Receipts carry hashes rather than the task text or the credential. When the invariant fires, the in-process tripwire result still includes the matched substring so the caller can act on it. The value that is signed is a detector name, a length, and a mask. The earlier defect, in which the signed evidence contained the raw substring while the redaction manifest claimed otherwise, does not reproduce on this revision.

\subsection{Adoption cost (DR5)}

Table~\ref{tab:overhead} and Figure~\ref{fig:overhead} report latency over \OverheadTrials{} trials after warm-up, against a mocked provider. Signing added \SignDeltaSmallMs{}~ms at the median for a 1~KB text input and \SignDeltaImageMs{}~ms for a 1~MB image (signed run minus unsigned run). Verification of the small-text receipt was \VerifySmallMs{}~ms at the median. The receipt grows with the number of input hashes rather than with payload size (Table~\ref{tab:size}). Linked receipts and chain verification scale with depth (Table~\ref{tab:chain}, Figure~\ref{fig:chain}). Because the provider was a zero-latency fake, these figures are the runtime's own cost, not a share of a hosted model's latency. We did not measure real provider latency.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Contribution to IS knowledge}

In terms of the knowledge contribution framework of \citet{gregor2013}, this study offers an improvement: a new solution to a known problem, auditable record keeping, for a new application context. Its contribution has two levels. At the level of nascent design theory \citep{gregor2007}, it contributes five requirements and four principles for run-level AI accountability that apply beyond the specific artifact. At the level of the instantiation, it contributes an open-source artifact and a reusable evaluation harness, together with quantitative evidence about which principles the instantiation realizes. The evaluation also yields design knowledge that is easy to overlook. A self-attested receipt guarantees integrity of what was signed, not freshness, unless the issuer set an expiry or the verifier asks for an audience, a nonce, or a maximum age. Linking receipts does not by itself reveal a missing tail. A closing receipt that lists members does. A redaction manifest is a claim unless the signed bytes are the masked form, which this revision measures. These lessons transfer to any organization that designs audit trails for AI-enabled processes.

The study also connects IS research on AI governance \citep{berente2021,mantymaki2022,papagiannidis2025} with the security literature on tamper-evident records \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009,laurie2013}. Governance research has described \emph{what} organizations must be able to show. This study demonstrates \emph{how} an information system can be designed so that the evidence is produced as a by-product of normal operation rather than as a separate compliance activity, extending the idea of sociotechnical envelopment \citep{asatiani2021} to the evidentiary layer.

\subsection{Implications for practice}

For managers and compliance officers, the findings suggest that run-level evidence can be produced at negligible runtime cost, which removes a common argument against comprehensive logging of AI use. Evidence of this kind maps onto record-keeping duties in the EU AI Act \citep{euaiact2024}, onto the measurement and management functions of the NIST AI RMF \citep{nist2023}, and onto the documented controls an ISO/IEC 42001 management system expects \citep{iso42001}. It can supply concrete artifacts for the application layer of LLM audits \citep{mokander2023}. Three cautions follow from the evaluation. First, an issuer-signed receipt proves integrity, not honesty. Organizations that need protection against a dishonest operator should combine receipts with receiver-side attestations or transparency logs, as in Sello \citep{figuera2026sello} or Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013}. Second, auditors should insist on chain verification and on the catalog version used for routing. Third, simple pattern-based personal-data checks should not be treated as a complete privacy control. On the in-scope labeled set they were precise, and they still miss types they do not claim, plus the occasional number that sits next to an identifier cue.

For developers, the design principles suggest a pattern: configure signing once, commit to hashes rather than content, make consequential decisions pure functions that can be recomputed, and verify before loading.

\subsection{Limitations and future research}

The evaluation has several limitations, which are also tracked publicly \citep{latticeissue21}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mocked providers.} All provider calls were simulated, so overhead relative to real latency, live replay drift, and provider-specific behavior were not measured.
\item \emph{Replay binding is opt-in.} Audience, nonce, and a raised version floor are not required for verification. A receipt with no \code{expiresAt} does not expire unless the caller passes \code{maxAgeMs}. There is no replay cache. Advisories warn. They do not reject.
\item \emph{Open chains can still be tail-truncated.} Without \code{expectedCount} or a closing member list, truncation is an advisory. Crews minted by this revision do write that list.
\item \emph{Signer failure is visible, not fail-closed by default.} If the signer throws, the run still succeeds and the result carries \code{receiptError} unless the caller selects throw-on-signer-error. A run with no signer emits no receipt.
\item \emph{Regex personal-data checks.} Detectors cover email, United States social security numbers, payment cards with a known issuer prefix, and United States phone numbers. Names, addresses, and non-United States formats are out of scope. An identifier cue suppresses lookalikes and can also suppress a real number.
\item \emph{must-cite is syntactic.} It does not decide whether a cited artifact supports the prose.
\item \emph{Self-attestation and synthetic data.} Receipts are signed by the issuing runtime. The policy evaluation used synthetic data.
\item \emph{No human evaluation yet} (Sect.~\ref{sec:eval-study}).
\end{itemize}
Future research can combine issuer receipts with receiver attestations and transparency logs, study how auditors use receipts in practice, and examine how receipts can serve as evidence in conformity assessments under emerging AI regulation.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

AI-enabled information systems need records that auditors can verify and operators can reproduce. Following design science research, this study derived requirements and principles for run-level AI accountability, instantiated them in Lattice, and evaluated the instantiation offline. Where the harness required rejection, single-receipt tamper attempts were rejected, recorded runs replayed to the signed output hash, routing policies were not violated, and signing added milliseconds per run (Table~\ref{tab:eval-map}). Freshness binding and open-chain length remain opt-in. Personal data matched by the privacy check is masked before signing. Together, the measured results and the remaining opt-ins are design knowledge for organizations that want AI evidence produced during the run.
