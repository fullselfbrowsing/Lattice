\begin{abstract}
\noindent Organizations embed large language models in business processes, yet auditors need evidence of which model served a run and what it produced. This design-science study states five requirements and four principles for run-level accountability and instantiates them in Lattice, an open-source runtime. When a signer is configured, single calls and multi-agent crews yield canonical receipts, signed after matched personal data is masked. A receipt commits to hashes of the input artifacts and the output, to the output contract, and to the selected route, but not to the task text, the policy, or the reasons for the route. An offline evaluation on mocked providers rejects tampering of single receipts, binds recorded outputs to the signed output hash, and adds milliseconds of signing cost. Freshness binding and open-chain length checks stay verifier opt-ins, and crew chains list their members. The study contributes design knowledge for accountable AI information systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from experimentation into operational information systems that approve refunds, triage claims, summarize case files, and draft regulated communications \citep{berente2021,bommasani2021}. These systems combine text, images, documents, and tool calls, and they often route a single task across several model providers for reasons of cost, latency, privacy, or capability \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm}. Their outputs therefore carry organizational consequences, and with consequences comes accountability: someone must be able to explain, after the fact, what the system was asked to do, which model served the request under which constraints, and what came back \citep{wieringa2020,novelli2023}.

Regulatory and standards frameworks make this expectation explicit. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act requires providers of high-risk systems to enable automatic recording of events over the system's lifetime and to keep such logs \citep{euaiact2024}. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework asks organizations to document and monitor AI systems so that risks can be measured and managed \citep{nist2023}, and ISO/IEC 42001 specifies a management system in which AI-related processes must be controlled and evidenced \citep{iso42001}. Scholarship on AI auditing reaches a similar conclusion: internal and external audits depend on artifacts that make claims about a system verifiable rather than merely asserted \citep{raji2020,brundage2020,mokander2021,mokander2023}.

Current practice falls short of this. Most organizations integrate models through provider wrappers and orchestration libraries that normalize request formats and add conveniences such as tool calling and tracing \citep{amershi2019,sculley2015}. Their traces are written for human debugging. A trace can be edited after the fact, it does not bind inputs to outputs, and a third party cannot check it without trusting the party that produced it \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009}. The result is an accountability gap between what governance frameworks expect and what AI-enabled information systems actually record \citep{raji2020,papagiannidis2025}.

This study addresses that gap through design science research (DSR) \citep{hevner2004,peffers2007,gregor2013}. The research question is: \emph{How should a model-run infrastructure be designed so that every run of an AI-enabled information system yields evidence that auditors can verify independently and operators can reproduce, at acceptable cost?} We derive design requirements from the governance and auditing literature, formulate design principles, instantiate them in an open-source artifact called Lattice \citep{lattice}, and evaluate the artifact against each requirement with a reproducible, offline evaluation harness. The central construct is the \emph{capability receipt}: a per-run record that is canonicalized, redacted before signing, signed with a public-key signature, and linked to content-addressed artifacts so that the record and the stored artifacts can be checked offline.

The study makes three contributions. First, it specifies requirements and principles for run-level AI accountability, a nascent design theory in the sense of \citet{gregor2007} that is independent of any particular implementation. Second, it provides an instantiation whose behavior is evaluated quantitatively, covering integrity under attack, binding of recorded outputs, policy enforcement, and runtime overhead. Third, it reports where the instantiation does \emph{not} meet its requirements, findings that are themselves design knowledge for builders of accountable AI systems \citep{baskerville2018}. We position the contribution as an \emph{improvement} in the sense of \citet{gregor2013}: a known problem, auditable record keeping, addressed with a new solution for a new class of systems, multi-provider model runs.

The remainder of the paper follows the DSR publication schema proposed by \citet{gregor2013}. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the research approach. Section~\ref{sec:requirements} derives requirements and principles. Section~\ref{sec:artifact} presents the artifact. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses contributions, implications, and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AI accountability, governance, and auditing}

Accountability in AI denotes the obligation of an actor to explain and justify the conduct of a system to a forum that can pass judgment and impose consequences \citep{wieringa2020,novelli2023}. IS research frames AI management as a matter of autonomy, learning, and inscrutability \citep{berente2021}, and it describes how organizations envelop AI with sociotechnical controls to contain its behavior \citep{asatiani2021}. Organizational AI governance comprises the structures, processes, and procedures that align AI use with organizational and regulatory goals \citep{mantymaki2022,schneider2022,papagiannidis2025}. Across these accounts, governance depends on evidence. Policies and controls mean little unless an organization can show, for specific decisions, that the controls operated.

The AI auditing literature makes this dependence concrete. \citet{raji2020} propose an end-to-end internal audit framework and stress documentation artifacts throughout the development life cycle. \citet{mokander2021} argue for ethics-based auditing as a governance mechanism, and \citet{mokander2023} propose a three-layered approach to auditing large language models at the governance, model, and application layers. \citet{brundage2020} call for mechanisms, including audit trails, that make claims about AI development verifiable by outsiders. Transparency research cautions that transparency must be designed for specific audiences and purposes \citep{felzmann2019,rai2020}, and human-centered AI calls for reliable, safe, and trustworthy systems backed by audit trails \citep{shneiderman2020}. Documentation instruments such as model cards \citep{mitchell2019} and datasheets for datasets \citep{gebru2021} standardize disclosure about models and data. However, they describe artifacts at release time, not individual production runs. The application layer of \citet{mokander2023}, where a model is used in a specific business process, is where the evidence gap is widest, and it is the layer this study targets.

\subsection{Tamper-evident logging, provenance, and transparency}

Computer security offers a long tradition of tamper-evident records. \citet{haber1991} showed how to time-stamp digital documents, and \citet{merkle1988} introduced hash-tree commitments. \citet{schneier1999} designed secure audit logs whose entries cannot be undetectably altered after compromise, and \citet{crosby2009} gave efficient data structures for tamper-evident logs with logarithmic proofs. Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013} publishes certificates to append-only logs so that misissuance becomes publicly detectable. Software supply chain research applies these ideas to builds. in-toto \citep{intoto} signs each step of a software supply chain, The Update Framework addresses survivable key compromise \citep{samuel2010}, Sigstore makes signing and transparency logging accessible to developers \citep{newman2022}, and SLSA defines levels of supply chain integrity \citep{slsa}. The Dead Simple Signing Envelope (DSSE) specifies a minimal signed envelope with a pre-authentication encoding \citep{dsse}. Data provenance research models the derivation history of data products \citep{moreau2011,herschel2017}.

These lines of work supply mechanisms (canonical encodings, signatures, hash chains, transparency logs) but were not designed for the semantics of a model run. A model run has a declared intent, a routing decision among providers, policy constraints, output invariants, and multimodal inputs that may contain personal data. Model gateways and observability libraries such as LiteLLM \citep{litellm}, LangChain callbacks \citep{langchain}, OpenLLMetry \citep{openllmetry}, and the Portkey gateway \citep{portkey} sit on that path and log the calls, but none of the four signs its log, and the logs that could be read stored the prompt text (Table~\ref{tab:rw-gateways}). Figure~\ref{fig:rw-gateway-latency} gives their latency on a CPU-only host, where generation noise dominates.

\subsection{Verifiable inference and model routing}

A complementary stream seeks cryptographic guarantees that a specific model computed a specific output, using zero-knowledge proofs \citep{kang2022zk,sun2024}. Such proofs give strong guarantees but currently require access to model weights and impose high costs, so they do not apply to proprietary hosted models consumed through application programming interfaces (APIs). Work on model routing optimizes cost and quality by selecting among models \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm}, often with learned routers whose decisions are hard to audit. Security research on LLM-integrated applications shows that model outputs can be manipulated through indirect prompt injection \citep{greshake2023}, which raises the stakes for run-level evidence.

\subsection{Agent receipts and verifiable agent provenance}

Three recent proposals are closest to this study. \emph{TrustTrack} is a vision paper that argues for verifiable identity, policy commitments, and tamper-resistant behavioral logs in multi-agent systems \citep{li2025trusttrack}. \emph{Sello} (``Notarized Agents'') inverts the trust boundary: the service that receives an agent's call signs a receipt, encrypts it to the agent's owner, and publishes it to a witness-cosigned Merkle log, so that the owner need not trust the agent or its operator \citep{figuera2026sello}. \emph{Janus} places a signed, hash-chained log on the effect path of agentic LLMs, so that a step's proposal and verdict are durable before its effect is released. Its gates are pure functions of the log and can be re-derived offline, and it is evaluated under crash injection and with a lending workflow \citep{arslan2026janus}. Two further systems are run in the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:realworld} but are not tabulated here: Agent Flight Recorder, which keeps tamper-evident audit trails of tool-using agents with on-chain anchoring \citep{bindschaedler2026afr}, and NovaFabric, which seals tamper-evident, replayable evidence for autonomous agent runs \citep{ardebili2026novafabric}.

Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares these systems with the artifact presented here. The comparison shows that the contribution of this study is not the idea of signed records for AI, which all four share, but a specific combination of design features aimed at \emph{organizational} model use: issuer-side receipts for single calls and crews that have a signer, a deterministic routing decision whose selected route is signed, redaction before signing, and signed output binding. The comparison also shows what Lattice lacks: Sello's receiver-side attestation and transparency log, Janus's effect gating and crash-consistency evaluation, and a commitment to the task text and the policy, which Lattice receipts do not carry (Section~\ref{sec:artifact}). Lattice was designed independently. Receipt code first appears in the project repository \citep{lattice} in commit \texttt{fa6d5a1} of 11 May 2026, before the Sello (June 2026) and Janus (September 2026) preprints appeared. Audience, nonce, and expiry binding, chain verification, and chain closure were added in October 2026, after both.

\section{Research Approach}\label{sec:method}

We follow the design science research methodology (DSRM) of \citet{peffers2007}, guided by the DSR guidelines of \citet{hevner2004} and the contribution framework of \citet{gregor2013}. Table~\ref{tab:dsrm} maps the six DSRM activities to this study. The entry point is problem-centered: the accountability gap arose while building a browser-automation product whose autonomous actions required an auditable record, and it generalizes to any organization that embeds models in business processes. DSR builds and evaluates artifacts to solve organizational problems while producing prescriptive knowledge \citep{vombrocke2020}, and it has long been linked to information systems design theories \citep{walls1992}. We use the anatomy of a design theory \citep{gregor2007} to structure the knowledge contribution, with requirements as meta-requirements, principles as principles of form and function, and Lattice as an expository instantiation. Evaluation follows the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS) of \citet{venable2016}. We adopt a technical risk and efficacy strategy, starting with artificial, summative evaluation of the instantiation against its requirements, and plan a naturalistic evaluation with practitioners as the next episode (Section~\ref{sec:eval-study}). Evaluation criteria are drawn from the taxonomy of \citet{prat2015} (goal attainment, efficiency, robustness, and consistency) and from the build-evaluate pattern of \citet{sonnenberg2012}.

\section{Design Requirements and Principles}\label{sec:requirements}

\subsection{Design requirements}

We derive five design requirements (DRs) from the governance, auditing, and secure logging literature. Each is stated at the level of the class of systems rather than of any particular implementation.

\textbf{DR1 (Integrity and attribution).} The record of each model run must be tamper-evident and attributable to an accountable issuer, so that any alteration is detectable by a party holding only public verification material. This follows from logging duties for high-risk systems \citep{euaiact2024}, from the verifiable-claims agenda \citep{brundage2020}, and from secure audit log research \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009}.

\textbf{DR2 (Reproducibility).} An auditor must be able to check offline, without access to the original system or provider, that the stored inputs and outputs of a run are the ones the record commits to. This reflects the role of reproducibility in audits \citep{raji2020} and in ML practice more broadly \citep{kapoor2023}.

\textbf{DR3 (Inspectable decisions and enforced policy).} The record must state which provider and model served the run, and enough of the request, the policy, and the provider catalog must be kept for an auditor to recompute that choice. Declared policies (for example, privacy class, provider restrictions, budget, or citation and personal-data constraints) must be enforced, with violations recorded rather than silently retried. This reflects the inscrutability concern in AI management \citep{berente2021}, the need for explanations tailored to auditors \citep{felzmann2019}, and risk-management functions in \citet{nist2023}.

\textbf{DR4 (Data minimization).} The record must not disclose secrets or personal data beyond what accountability requires, so that it can be shared with auditors without creating a new privacy risk. This reflects data protection obligations and the transparency--privacy tension discussed by \citet{felzmann2019}.

\textbf{DR5 (Low adoption cost).} Producing the record must add negligible runtime overhead and require little change to existing application code, because accountability mechanisms that impose high costs are bypassed in practice \citep{sculley2015,amershi2019}.

\subsection{Design principles}

We formulate four design principles (DPs), following the convention that a principle names the action, the mechanism, and the rationale \citep{gregor2013}.

\textbf{DP1 (Receipt by default).} Provide the runtime with a signing key so that \emph{every} run, whether successful or failed, emits a receipt that is canonicalized, signed, and verifiable with a public key, because accountability evidence that depends on developer discipline is incomplete. (Addresses DR1, DR5.) Lattice realizes DP1 for single calls (\code{ai.run}) and for multi-agent crews (\code{runAgentCrew}), but not for its single-agent loop (Table~\ref{tab:dp-mechanism}).

\textbf{DP2 (Commit to content, carry hashes).} Have the receipt commit to content-addressed hashes of input artifacts and outputs rather than to the content itself, and keep the content in a separate artifact store, because this makes receipts small and shareable while still allowing stored content to be checked against the record. (Addresses DR2, DR4, DR5.)

\textbf{DP3 (Decide deterministically and record the decision).} Implement provider and model selection as a pure function of a capability catalog, the request, and the policy, and record the selected route in the receipt, because a decision that can be recomputed can be audited. Treat output-policy violations as terminal, so that a run is never retried until a banned output passes. (Addresses DR3.)

\textbf{DP4 (Redact before signing, verify before loading).} Apply redaction before canonicalization and signing, so that matched sensitive values never enter the signed bytes in clear form, and verify a receipt before loading any referenced artifact, so that a forged receipt cannot trigger side effects. (Addresses DR1, DR2, DR4.) A hash in the signed bytes hides a value only when that value is hard to guess. A low-entropy value, such as a social security number or a yes/no output, can be confirmed from its unsalted hash by dictionary search. Salted or keyed commitments would close this gap and are left to future work.

\paragraph{Receipt contents.} A signed receipt body holds the run identifier, the issue time, the signing key identifier, the requested and observed model, the selected route (provider, capability, and attempt number), token usage and cost, the contract verdict, the hash of the contract, the SHA-256 hashes of the input artifacts and of the output, a Merkle root over artifact lineage, and the redaction manifest. When a tripwire fires, the body also holds redacted tripwire evidence, and when no route exists, it lists the rejection reasons. Optional fields bind an audience, a nonce, an expiry, a parent receipt, and chain closure. The body neither contains nor hashes the task text, the packed prompt, or the policy, and it records the selected route without the candidates, scores, or catalog version that produced it. Two runs that differ only in their task text, and that return the same output with the same token usage, therefore yield receipts that differ only in identifiers and time. DR2 and DR3 hold for what the receipt commits to, not for these omitted inputs.

\paragraph{Provider coverage.} Seven provider adapters (OpenAI, generic OpenAI-compatible endpoints, Anthropic, Google Gemini, xAI, OpenRouter, and LM Studio for local models) implement a common contract, so a receipt has the same fields whichever provider served the run. What reaches the provider differs. The Anthropic and Gemini adapters send image artifacts as native image parts. The OpenAI, OpenAI-compatible, xAI, OpenRouter, and LM Studio adapters send every artifact as a text part holding JSON. A data-URL string is inlined as text, and binary image bytes are dropped from the request, yet the receipt still commits to the hash of the dropped input. On those five adapters a receipt can therefore list an input the model never saw. The vision probe in Section~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} shows the effect. The router consults a capability catalog generated from a public model registry, supplemented by hand-maintained profiles.

\paragraph{Agent runs.} With a signer, \code{runAgentCrew} returns a crew-start root, a completion receipt for the root agent and for each child run that completed, and a crew-close receipt, so a crew of $N$ agents that each run once returns $N+2$ receipts. Each receipt after the root links to the root by its content identifier. The crew-start output hash commits to the nested tree of agent identifiers. It does not cover the agents' intents, tools, or policies. Completion receipts hash each agent's output. The root declares \code{chainClosure}, and the crew-close receipt hashes and lists the content identifiers of every other member, so \code{verifyChain} rejects a closed chain with a dropped leaf or a dropped closer. Verifiers in the 1.6.x line accept these receipts but do not enforce closure. Later verifiers enforce it. Two verifier options extend the check: \code{requireClosure} fails a chain that declares no closure, and \code{partial} accepts a disclosed subset only when the closer is present and lists every presented receipt. The crew budget verdict is fixed when agent work ends, before the root agent's completion receipt and the crew-close receipt are signed, so signing those two receipts cannot push a crew over budget, and the crew-close receipt signs a failed verdict when the crew exceeded its budget. The crew-start and child completion receipts are signed during the run and count against its wall time. If a mint after the crew-start receipt fails, the result lists every failed mint in \code{receiptError} by default, and the crew throws at the failed mint when \code{onSignerError} is set to throw. A missing closer leaves the closed chain unverifiable. The single-agent loop \code{ai.runAgent} returns no receipt (Table~\ref{tab:dp-mechanism}).

\paragraph{External executors.} An organization that already uses another orchestration framework can call an audit helper that mints the same receipt family from externally produced request, response, and output data. This supports incremental adoption (DR5), at the price of trusting the external executor's account of the provider call.

\paragraph{Command-line tool.} The tool verifies receipts and receipt chains, prints structured advisories as warnings or information lines, checks stored artifacts and outputs offline against the signed hashes, compares receipts structurally, and gates regressions in cost and output consistency across a corpus of receipts in continuous integration. The library can also export run events through OpenTelemetry \citep{otel}, and tool results from the Model Context Protocol \citep{mcp} can be recorded as artifacts.

\subsection{Integrity under attack (DR1)}

We generated \TamperBase{} receipts from runs across all seven adapters and applied the mutations in Table~\ref{tab:tamper}. Of the cases the harness classifies as attacks, \TamperRejected{} of \TamperAttacks{} were rejected. Body edits failed signature verification whether the attacker kept the original signature, re-canonicalized correctly, or re-signed with an untrusted key under the legitimate key identifier. Non-canonical encodings were rejected as canonicalization mismatches. A valid receipt presented a second time is accepted: the verifier keeps no replay cache. Audience, nonce, and a caller-supplied version floor are checked only when requested. \code{expiresAt}, when the issuer set it, is enforced on every verification. Presenting the same receipt again is therefore not, by itself, a failed verification. What the verifier did not check is reported as structured advisories, each with a code and a severity. A warning means the receipt is bound to an audience or a nonce that the caller did not check. An information advisory means the receipt carries no audience, nonce, or expiry, or sits below the current schema version. The command-line verifier always prints warnings and prints information lines unless \code{--quiet} is set. Figure~\ref{fig:rw-tamper-heat} applies comparable mutation classes to the other record systems of Section~\ref{sec:realworld}.

Chain checks are separate from the ten \code{VerifyErrorKind} values. \code{verifyChain} reports its own error kinds (\code{VerifyChainErrorKind}): an invalid link, no root, multiple roots, an orphan link, a run-identifier mismatch, a duplicate content identifier, a count mismatch, or a root mismatch. Reordering the receipt array is not an error, because the verifier follows parent links rather than array position. Dropping a middle link, splicing a foreign link, or replacing the root fails that check. Truncating the tail of an open chain does not. The result is success plus a tail-truncation warning unless the caller passes \code{expectedCount}. A chain whose root declares \code{chainClosure} fails with a count mismatch when a leaf or the closing receipt is missing, with no expected count, and a malformed closing receipt fails as an invalid link. \code{requireClosure} turns a missing closure declaration into a failure, and \code{partial} accepts a disclosed subset only when the closing receipt is present and lists every presented receipt, and warns when some listed members were not presented. Table~\ref{tab:v16} records these checks together with the advisories, crew receipts, and signer-failure modes. \VerifierChecksPassed{} of \VerifierChecks{} checks passed. Two of them run the released 1.6.0 verifier on a closed crew chain and confirm that it accepts the chain without enforcing closure.

\subsection{Reproducibility (DR2)}

For each of the seven adapters we recorded runs against provider-shaped fixtures, wrote the receipt and content-addressed artifacts to disk, reloaded them, verified the receipt, materialized a replay envelope, replayed offline, and recomputed the output hash. \ReplayVerified{} of \ReplayRuns{} runs verified, and \ReplayHash{} reproduced the signed output hash. Re-executing each run against the same fixture matched in \ReplayLive{} cases, and a one-character change to a recorded output was detected in \ReplayAltered{} cases (Table~\ref{tab:replay}). These results show that the record is internally consistent. Offline replay does not call a model: \code{replayOffline} returns the outputs the caller supplies, and the check is that they hash to the signed output hash. The result is therefore a signed output binding, which other signed record systems also provide (Table~\ref{tab:rw-matrix}). The same binding held on every live call that returned a receipt (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-replay}). It does not show that a live model would reproduce its output, and repeated live calls often returned different answers (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-drift}). The binding covers input artifacts and outputs only. Because the task text is not committed, a receipt does not show which instruction produced the output. In a crew chain, the crew-start receipt hashes the nested agent tree (\code{\{kind: "crew-start", agents: \{id, children\}\}}), each completion receipt hashes its agent's output, and the crew-close receipt hashes its member list.

\subsection{Inspectable decisions and policy enforcement (DR3)}\label{sec:eval-policy}

\paragraph{Routing.} Using a synthetic catalog of 40 model capabilities and \RouteRequests{} random requests with random policies, repeated decisions were identical at rate \RouteIdentical{}. An independently implemented oracle found \RouteViolations{} selected routes that violated provider allow or deny lists, privacy class, logging, latency, or image-modality constraints, and \RouteFalseNo{} cases in which the router reported no route although a feasible candidate existed. Reordering the catalog changed the selected model at a same-selection rate of \RoutePermSame{}. Catalog position is not part of the score, and ties break on provider and model identifiers. The receipt signs the selected route and the requested and observed model. It does not sign the policy, the candidate list, the scores, or the catalog version, so an auditor who wants to recompute the decision needs those from records kept outside the receipt.

\paragraph{Personal-data invariant.} The \code{noPII} detectors cover four types, and each rule trades recall for precision in a stated way. Email addresses match as written. A dashed United States social security number (SSN) matches as written. Spaced and undashed nine-digit forms match only when an SSN cue such as \code{ssn}, \code{ss\#}, or \code{social security} is the nearest word. Separated United States phone numbers match as written. A plain ten-digit run matches only when it is a valid North American number and the nearest word is a phone cue (\code{phone}, \code{tel}, \code{call}, \code{mobile}, \code{cell}, or \code{fax}) or a \code{+1} trunk prefix precedes it. A payment card number is any Luhn-valid run of 13 to 19 digits, except runs that start with 0 or 1 and 13-digit runs that start with 978 or 979, which are ISBN-13 numbers. An SSN, phone, or card match is suppressed when an identifier label (\code{order}, \code{ord}, \code{invoice}, \code{inv}, \code{sku}, \code{ref}, \code{po}, \code{isbn}, \code{pn}, or \code{txn}) is the nearest token on the same line, joined to the number only by whitespace or by \code{:}, \code{\#}, \code{=}, \code{no.}, \code{number}, or \code{is}, unless the number is a phone number with a trunk prefix. Labels do not suppress email addresses. Words such as customer, account, and ticket are not labels. The trade-offs follow from these rules. A real SSN written as \code{order 123-45-6789} is not flagged. A bare nine- or ten-digit number without a type cue is not flagged, whether or not it is personal data. About one in ten random digit strings passes the Luhn check, so a text that lists $k$ numeric identifiers of 13 to 19 digits is flagged as holding a card with probability near $1-0.9^{k}$. The detectors see only strings, so a value stored under a key such as \code{ssn} is judged by its digits alone.

We built a labeled set of synthetic strings, 100 per label. In-scope positives cover each form the detectors claim, support-ticket text in which a number follows words such as customer, account holder, or patient ID, CSV and markdown rows, and card numbers from several networks. Out-of-scope personal data are names with addresses, international bank account numbers, United Kingdom phone numbers, IP addresses, dates of birth, and spelled-out email addresses. Negatives include order identifiers and stock-keeping units shaped like SSNs or phone numbers, 16-digit references, ISBN-13 numbers, and uncued numbers such as timestamps, byte counts, and bank routing numbers. On in-scope types plus all negatives, \code{noPII} reached precision \PiiInPrecision{} and recall \PiiInRecall{} (\(n=\PiiInN\), false positives \PiiInFp{}, false negatives \PiiInFn{}). The false negatives come from forms the rules leave out by design, such as a plain ten-digit number after ``call the customer at'' and an undashed SSN in a CSV row with no SSN cue. The \PiiNewNegN{} uncued numeric negatives produced \PiiNewNegFp{} false positives. Counting every personal-data label, including types the detectors do not claim, precision was \PiiAllPrecision{} and recall \PiiAllRecall{} (Table~\ref{tab:policy}, Figure~\ref{fig:pii}). The cases were chosen with the rules in view, and the identifier-shaped negatives carry the labels the rules key on, so these figures show that the rules behave as specified. They are not prevalence-weighted estimates and not estimates of precision on real text. On public datasets, SSN and phone precision and recall were far lower, because passport numbers and other identifiers share the same digit shapes (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}, Figure~\ref{fig:rw-pii}, Tables~\ref{tab:rw-pii-ai4} and~\ref{tab:rw-pii-gretel}, Figures~\ref{fig:rw-pii-f1} and~\ref{fig:rw-pii-latency}). Those public-data rows were measured with the rules at commit \texttt{a8cc091}, which differ from the rules above as Section~\ref{sec:realworld} describes.

\paragraph{Citation invariant.} The \code{mustCite} invariant checks that an output cites a named artifact. By default it reads only structured lists: a top-level \code{citations} or \code{evidence} list whose string entries, or whose objects' \code{source} field, name the artifact. Matching is case-sensitive unless case folding is requested, and other object keys can be named. If the top-level list is missing or omits the artifact, nested \code{citations} and \code{evidence} lists are searched, unless the contract declares a path. The default never reads prose. Three options extend it, and each is added to the contract only when set, so the hash of an existing contract does not change. With \code{inline}, a bracketed or parenthesized group in the prose whose content, or one comma- or semicolon-separated part of it, equals the artifact name also counts; it does not override a non-empty structured list that omits the artifact. With \code{rejectUncited}, a matching list entry is rejected when the prose contains citation-like square brackets, such as \code{[1]}, \code{[1-3]}, or \code{[Smith 1999]}, and none of them resolves to the artifact. Links, footnote markers, and code are not treated as citations. \code{textPath} limits these prose checks to one field. The invariant agreed with the labels in \MustCiteAgree{} of \MustCiteCases{} constructed cases: \MustCiteDefaultAgree{} of \MustCiteDefaultCases{} for the default and \MustCiteOptInAgree{} of \MustCiteOptInCases{} for the options. The cases include numeric markers, links, and path-scoped outputs, and they were also chosen with the implementation in view. On ASQA long answers, which are prose without citation lists, it rarely accepted a gold title (Section~\ref{sec:realworld}). The check is syntactic. It does not judge whether the cited artifact supports the claim \citep{lewis2020rag}.

\subsection{Data minimization (DR4)}\label{sec:eval-min}

We placed marker secrets in the task, an input artifact, the provider credential, and the model output, then scanned the decoded signed payload, with and without a \code{noPII} invariant. Marker hits in signed bytes were \LeakHits{} of \LeakRows{} rows (Table~\ref{tab:policy} does not repeat the per-adapter log; the raw rows are in \code{eval/results/policy.json}). Receipts carry hashes of the input artifacts and the output. The task text and the credential appear in the signed bytes neither in clear nor as a hash. When the invariant fires, the in-process tripwire result still includes the matched substring so the caller can act on it. The value that is signed is a detector name, a length, and a mask, so the signed bytes hold the masked form that the redaction manifest describes. The scan checks for the marker strings themselves. It does not test whether a hash can be inverted: a low-entropy input or output, such as an SSN or a yes/no answer, can be confirmed from its unsalted hash by dictionary search (Section~\ref{sec:requirements}, DP4). Among the record systems compared in Section~\ref{sec:realworld}, Figure~\ref{fig:rw-bytes} shows which kept the plaintext transcript and which kept only digests or ciphertext when wrapping the same recorded request and response bytes.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Contribution to IS knowledge}

In terms of the knowledge contribution framework of \citet{gregor2013}, this study offers an improvement: a new solution to a known problem, auditable record keeping, for a new application context. Its contribution has two levels. At the level of nascent design theory \citep{gregor2007}, it contributes five requirements and four principles for run-level AI accountability that apply beyond the specific artifact. At the level of the instantiation, it contributes an open-source artifact and a reusable evaluation harness, together with quantitative evidence about which principles the instantiation realizes. The evaluation also yields design knowledge that is easy to overlook. A self-attested receipt guarantees integrity of what was signed, not freshness, unless the issuer set an expiry or the verifier asks for an audience, a nonce, or a maximum age. A receipt attests only to what is hashed into it. Lattice receipts omit the task text and the policy, so they show what came back and from which route, not what the system was asked. A hash of an input does not show that the provider received the input, as the dropped images on OpenAI-shaped adapters show, and a hash hides a value only when the value is hard to guess. Linking receipts does not by itself reveal a missing tail. A closing receipt that lists members does, but only for verifiers that enforce it. A redaction manifest is a claim unless the signed bytes are the masked form, which the leakage scan checks. Offline replay of stored outputs is an output binding, not a re-execution, and other signed record systems offer the same binding (Figure~\ref{fig:rw-capability}). These lessons transfer to any organization that designs audit trails for AI-enabled processes.

The study also connects IS research on AI governance \citep{berente2021,mantymaki2022,papagiannidis2025} with the security literature on tamper-evident records \citep{schneier1999,crosby2009,laurie2013}. Governance research has described \emph{what} organizations must be able to show. This study demonstrates \emph{how} an information system can be designed so that the evidence is produced as a by-product of normal operation rather than as a separate compliance activity, extending the idea of sociotechnical envelopment \citep{asatiani2021} to the evidentiary layer.

\subsection{Implications for practice}

For managers and compliance officers, the findings suggest that run-level evidence can be produced at negligible runtime cost, which removes a common argument against comprehensive logging of AI use. Evidence of this kind maps onto record-keeping duties in the EU AI Act \citep{euaiact2024}, onto the measurement and management functions of the NIST AI RMF \citep{nist2023}, and onto the documented controls an ISO/IEC 42001 management system expects \citep{iso42001}. It can supply concrete artifacts for the application layer of LLM audits \citep{mokander2023}. Three cautions follow from the evaluation. First, an issuer-signed receipt proves integrity, not honesty. Organizations that need protection against a dishonest operator should combine receipts with receiver-side attestations or transparency logs, as in Sello \citep{figuera2026sello} or Certificate Transparency \citep{laurie2013}. Second, auditors should insist on chain verification and on the catalog version used for routing, and should keep the task text and the policy alongside the receipt, because the receipt does not commit to them. Third, pattern-based personal-data checks are not a complete privacy control. On the synthetic set they behave as specified, but on public datasets their SSN and phone precision and recall were low (Tables~\ref{tab:rw-pii-ai4} and~\ref{tab:rw-pii-gretel}), because other identifiers share the same digit shapes. They also miss types they do not claim, numbers written after an identifier label, and bare numbers without a type cue.

For developers, the design principles suggest a pattern: configure signing once, commit to hashes rather than content, make consequential decisions pure functions that can be recomputed, and verify before loading.

\subsection{Limitations and future research}

The evaluation has several limitations. Some of them were first reported in an evaluation filed as a public issue \citep{latticeissue21}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mocked and live providers.} The controlled tables use a simulated provider. Sections~\ref{sec:realworld} and~\ref{sec:realworld-broad} run a local model and free hosted models. The hosted sample is a free tier: it rate-limits, it does not represent paid latency, and one catalogued model rejects ordinary chat calls. The OpenAI-compatible adapter used for the live runs sends no temperature or token cap, so a fetch wrapper added both. Provider-time differences between a packed prompt and a one-line prompt are too noisy to attribute to the extra tokens.
\item \emph{The task, prompt, and policy are not signed.} A receipt commits to input-artifact hashes, the output hash, the contract hash, and the selected route. Two runs that differ only in their task text, and that return the same output with the same token usage, yield receipts that differ only in identifiers and time. The receipt does not record why a route was chosen.
\item \emph{The single-agent loop returns no receipt.} \code{ai.run} and \code{runAgentCrew} return receipts. \code{ai.runAgent} does not; with a signer on its intent, its checkpoint receipts reach only a configured tracer, and without a tracer they are discarded.
\item \emph{Artifact transport on OpenAI-shaped adapters.} The OpenAI, OpenAI-compatible, xAI, OpenRouter, and LM Studio adapters send artifacts as text parts holding JSON. A data-URL string is inlined as text, binary image bytes are dropped from the request, and the receipt still hashes the dropped input. In the vision probe of Section~\ref{sec:realworld-broad}, the Lattice arm sent the image as inlined text and misread a solid red image that a direct image request read correctly. Only the Anthropic and Gemini adapters send native image parts.
\item \emph{Unsalted hashes.} Input and output hashes are unsalted SHA-256, so a holder of a receipt can confirm a guessed low-entropy value, such as an SSN or a yes/no decision.
\item \emph{Freshness binding is opt-in.} Audience, nonce, and a raised version floor are not required for verification. A receipt with no \code{expiresAt} does not expire unless the caller passes \code{maxAgeMs}. There is no replay cache. Advisories report unchecked bindings as warnings or information. They do not reject.
\item \emph{Open chains can still be tail-truncated.} Without \code{expectedCount} or a closing member list, truncation produces a warning, not a failure. Crew chains carry a closing member list, but verifiers in the 1.6.x line do not enforce it.
\item \emph{Signer failure is visible, not fail-closed by default.} If the signer throws, the run still returns its result, which carries \code{receiptError}, unless \code{onSignerError} is set to throw. A crew-start signing failure always throws, because no agent work has run. A run with no signer emits no receipt.
\item \emph{Regex personal-data checks.} Detectors cover email, United States SSNs, payment cards, and United States phone numbers. Names, addresses, and non-United States formats are out of scope. An identifier label hides a real number written after it, bare nine- and ten-digit numbers need a type cue, and lists of long numeric identifiers can trip the card check. The public-data rows were measured with the rules at commit \texttt{a8cc091}, not with the rules described in Section~\ref{sec:eval-policy}.
\item \emph{must-cite is syntactic.} By default it reads only structured citation lists. Prose checks are opt-in. It does not decide whether a cited artifact supports the prose.
\item \emph{Self-attestation and synthetic data.} Receipts are signed by the issuing runtime. The policy evaluation used synthetic data, and its labeled cases were chosen with the rules they test in view.
\item \emph{No human evaluation yet} (Sect.~\ref{sec:eval-study}).
\end{itemize}
Future research can commit the task, the packed prompt, and the policy in the receipt, replace unsalted hashes with salted or keyed commitments, send images to OpenAI-shaped providers as native parts or record when an artifact could not be sent, combine issuer receipts with receiver attestations and transparency logs, study how auditors use receipts in practice, and examine how receipts can serve as evidence in conformity assessments under emerging AI regulation.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

AI-enabled information systems need records that auditors can verify and operators can reproduce. Following design science research, this study derived requirements and principles for run-level AI accountability, instantiated them in Lattice, and evaluated the instantiation offline. Where the harness required rejection, single-receipt tamper attempts were rejected, recorded outputs matched the signed output hash, routing policies were not violated, and signing added milliseconds per run (Table~\ref{tab:eval-map}). Freshness binding and open-chain length remain opt-in, and crew chains carry a closing member list. Personal data matched by the privacy check is masked before signing. The receipts do not commit to the task text or the policy, the single-agent loop returns none, and OpenAI-shaped adapters can hash an image the provider never received. Together, the measured results, the remaining opt-ins, and these gaps are design knowledge for organizations that want AI evidence produced during the run.
